% ---- ABSTRACT --------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Abstract}
% ~150--300 words. State the question, the data/approach, the key findings,
% and why they matter.
Correlative species distribution models for sessile marine invertebrates generally assume unrestricted larval access to all suitable habitat, yet propagule supply is computable from biophysical transport models and is rarely tested as a predictor. We combined a 7{,}468-station survey of the common cockle \textit{Cerastoderma edule} in the Limfjorden, Denmark (2018--2025), with a particle-tracking simulation of larval dispersal to ask whether connectivity metrics improve a habitat-based spatial GLMM of cockle standing stock. A spatial random field accounted for 61\% of the total variance in biomass and the environmental predictors --- depth, salinity, temperature, oxygen and shear stress --- for 16\%, whereas five alternative connectivity metrics added only 1.0--2.9\%, the smallest explained component in every model. Connectivity did improve fit ($\Delta$AIC = 11.6), but entered with consistently negative coefficients (in-strength $\hat{\beta} = -0.41$, 95\% CI $-0.63$ to $-0.18$), opposite in sign to a larval-subsidy expectation, and had almost no marginal association with biomass ($|\rho| < 0.12$ for every metric). Modelled supply was strongly collinear with the fjord's habitat geography ($\rho = 0.59$ with depth, $-0.67$ with salinity), so the best-connected cells are the deep, brackish inner basins in which cockles were least abundant. The result was insensitive to which year of the dispersal simulation was used, but two purely topological metrics reversed sign when the spatial field was removed. \ml{[Optional, if the co-authors want the species-sorting argument: independent genetic and agent-based-modelling evidence that the Limfjorden is a single well-connected unit would support an interpretation in which post-settlement processes, not larval delivery, set adult distribution.]} Rather than a case against connectivity in distribution models, we present two computable diagnostics --- the collinearity of modelled supply with the environmental predictors, and the stability of connectivity coefficients to a spatial random field --- that indicate whether a connectivity covariate can inform a distribution model in a given system.

\subsection{Larval transport and connectivity metrics}

Larval dispersal was simulated with a biophysical individual-based model that
couples a hydrodynamic ocean model with Lagrangian particle tracking and a
larval pelagic-duration/behaviour module \citep{yourABMref}. The study area was
discretized into 2x2 km grid cells. Virtual larvae were released from each cell and tracked until settlement; aggregating over all release events yielded a connectivity matrix $C$, where $C_{jk}$ is the number of larvae released in cell $j$ that settled in cell $k$, together with a release vector $R_j$.

We converted these into a flow matrix $F$ of settlement probabilities,
\begin{equation}
F_{jk} = \frac{C_{jk}}{R_j}, \qquad 0 \le \sum_{k} F_{jk} \le 1,
\end{equation}
so that $F_{jk}$ is the probability that a larva released in source cell $j$
settles in sink cell $k$ (rows index sources, columns sinks). We treated $F$ as
the weighted adjacency matrix of a directed dispersal network.

For each cell $k$ we computed the weighted in-strength, local retention, and
eigenvector centrality,
\begin{align}
I_k &= \sum_{j} F_{jk}, \\
L_k &= F_{kk}, \\
v_k &: \; \mathbf{v}\ \text{the leading eigenvector of}\ F,\ \ \max_k v_k = 1,
\end{align}

\subsection{Species distribution models}

We related cockle occurrence and biomass to environmental conditions, sampling gear, and larval connectivity using spatial generalized linear mixed models fitted with the \texttt{sdmTMB} package \citep{anderson2024} in \textsf{R}.For each survey location $i$ we modelled two responses: the presence--absence indicator $z_i\in\{0,1\}$ and the biomass density $y_i\ge 0$ (g\,m$^{-2}$).

Presence was modelled with a Bernoulli distribution and a logit link,
\begin{equation}
z_i \sim \mathrm{Bernoulli}(\pi_i), \qquad \operatorname{logit}(\pi_i) = \eta_i ,
\end{equation}
and biomass with a Tweedie distribution (compound Poisson--gamma, power
parameter $1<p<2$) and a log link,
\begin{equation}
y_i \sim \operatorname{Tweedie}(\mu_i,\phi,p), \qquad \log(\mu_i) = \eta_i .
\end{equation}

Both responses shared the same linear predictor,
\begin{equation}
\eta_i = \beta_0 + \gamma_{g(i)} + \sum_{k=1}^{5}\beta_k\,x_{ik} + \theta\,c_i + \omega_{s_i},
\end{equation}
where $\gamma_{g(i)}$ is the gear effect, $x_{ik}$ are the five (standardized)
environmental covariates, $c_i$ is connectivity, and $\omega_{s_i}$ is a spatial
random field.

% ---- RESULTS ---------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Results}

The survey comprised 7{,}468 stations sampled between 2018 and 2025, falling
within 324 of the 2$\times$2~km grid cells; cockles were present at 17.7\% of
stations.

\subsection{Space and habitat dominate}

Model selection was unambiguous about the ranking of the three blocks
(Table~\ref{tab:model-selection}). Removing the spatial random field was by far
the most costly simplification: the best non-spatial model was 1{,}125.2 AIC
units worse than the best spatial one. Removing the environmental block cost
125.6 units, and removing connectivity cost 11.6 units. The best-supported
biomass model was Space + Environment + Connectivity with closeness centrality
as the connectivity term, with in-strength 1.2 units behind; for presence the
in-strength model was best. All 36 fitted models converged.

Within the best biomass model, biomass fell with depth
($\hat{\beta} = -1.12 \pm 0.14$ per SD) and with maximum shear stress
($\hat{\beta} = -1.52 \pm 0.18$), and rose with salinity
($\hat{\beta} = 1.39 \pm 0.44$); the oxygen effect was weaker
($\hat{\beta} = -0.54 \pm 0.24$) and temperature was indistinguishable from zero
($\hat{\beta} = 0.15 \pm 0.36$). The densest beds therefore sat in the shallow,
saline, low-energy parts of the fjord. The spatial field had a Mat\'ern range of
5.4~km, i.e.\ residual spatial structure decayed over a distance of roughly two
to three grid cells.

Variance partitioning (Table~\ref{tab:variance-partitioning},
Fig.~\ref{fig:variance}) put the conditional $R^2$ at 84.5\% and the marginal
(fixed-effects) $R^2$ at 22.5\% on average across metrics. For the in-strength
model the spatial field accounted for 61.2\% of total variance, environment
16.2\%, survey gear 4.7\% and connectivity 1.8\%, with 16.2\% unexplained. This
ordering was stable across all five connectivity metrics (spatial field
60.3--64.9\%, environment 15.1--17.0\%, connectivity 1.0--2.9\%).

\subsection{Connectivity contributes little, and with the opposite sign}

Connectivity improved the model, but modestly and in the direction opposite to a
larval-subsidy expectation. All five metrics entered the biomass model with
negative coefficients whose confidence intervals excluded zero
(Fig.~\ref{fig:coefficients}): closeness centrality
$\hat{\beta} = -0.84$ (95\% CI $-1.30$, $-0.39$), in-degree $-0.63$ ($-1.15$,
$-0.10$), eigenvector centrality $-0.53$ ($-0.90$, $-0.16$), in-strength $-0.41$
($-0.63$, $-0.18$), and biomass in-strength (log) $-0.39$ ($-0.62$, $-0.16$).
Presence behaved the same way (in-strength $-0.31$; $-0.51$, $-0.11$). Higher
modelled larval supply was thus associated with \emph{lower} adult cockle
biomass, not higher.

The effect was also small relative to everything else in the model: at
1.0--2.9\% of total variance, connectivity was the smallest explained component
in every fitted model, below even the survey-gear intercept. The five metrics
were near-tied on AIC (a spread of 8.0 units across all five), so the data do not
distinguish between them.

\subsection{Supply and habitat share the same axis}
\label{sec:variance_partitioning}

The negative sign is intelligible once the collinearity between connectivity and
the fjord's habitat geography is made explicit. In-strength correlated with depth
($\rho = 0.59$), salinity ($\rho = -0.67$) and maximum shear stress
($\rho = -0.57$), and the other metrics behaved similarly (depth $\rho = 0.51$ to
$0.63$; salinity $\rho = -0.48$ to $-0.75$). Well-connected cells are the deep,
brackish, low-energy inner basins --- precisely the conditions under which
cockles were least abundant. Connectivity terms carried variance inflation
factors of 1.7--2.3 in the combined models, so the blocks remain separable, but
their contributions are only partly identifiable.

Consistent with this, connectivity had essentially no marginal association with
biomass: Spearman correlations at the survey stations ranged from $-0.081$
(in-strength) to $0.024$ (in-degree), i.e.\ $|\rho| < 0.12$ for every metric. The
negative effect reported above is therefore entirely conditional on the
environmental and spatial terms, and is best read as residual habitat structure
that the linear environmental terms do not fully absorb, rather than as evidence
that larval delivery suppresses adult biomass.

\subsection{Sensitivity to the dispersal year and to the spatial field}

The result did not depend on which year of the dispersal simulation was used.
Recomputing every metric from each annual flow matrix (2010--2016) and
correlating it with observed biomass gave correlations spanning only 0.087--0.137
across years, with the pooled matrix used in the fitted models lying inside that
band for every metric (Fig.~\ref{fig:year_sensitivity}). Closeness centrality was
the most stable metric across years (median coefficient of variation 0.02) and
eigenvector centrality the least (0.68).

Dependence on the spatial field was more consequential
(Fig.~\ref{fig:space_confounding}). Refitting without the field left the sign of
in-strength ($-0.41 \rightarrow -0.30$), biomass in-strength
($-0.39 \rightarrow -0.34$) and eigenvector centrality
($-0.53 \rightarrow -0.44$) unchanged, with magnitudes shrinking by 15--25\%. For
in-degree ($-0.63 \rightarrow +0.30$) and closeness centrality
($-0.84 \rightarrow +0.36$), however, the coefficient reversed sign. The two
purely topological metrics are therefore not separable from broad spatial
structure, whereas the supply-based metrics are. Dropping the field also
redistributed variance dramatically, with the survey-gear share rising from
roughly 5\% to 44\% and environment from 16\% to 26--30\%
(Fig.~\ref{fig:variance_nospace}), indicating that gear and location are partly
aliased in this survey and that the spatial field is what separates them.
