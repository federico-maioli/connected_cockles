\section*{Abstract}
Correlative species distribution models (SDMs) for marine invertebrates with
pelagic larvae usually rely on environmental predictors alone, implicitly assuming that larvae reach all suitable habitat. Larval supply is not uniform, however, and whether it adds to habitat in explaining where populations occur and how abundant they are has rarely been tested. We combined 8,137 cockle (\textit{Cerastoderma} spp.) survey stations from the Limfjorden, Denmark (2018--2025) with a biophysical model of larval dispersal in spatial delta-gamma SDMs containing environmental covariates, connectivity and a spatial random field, and assessed both model fit and predictive performance using spatially blocked cross-validation. Simulated larvae reached every surveyed station, yet cockles occurred at only 19\% of them. Habitat explained where they occurred, with occurrence peaking at about 7 m depth, increasing with salinity and declining with temperature and bottom shear stress. Connectivity added little explanatory or predictive power beyond
environmental covariates and spatial structure, and was highest where habitat was least suitable. Among occupied beds, biomass reflected fine-scale spatial structure rather than habitat or connectivity. Habitat therefore set where cockles occurred, while variation in how much biomass they supported was structured at finer scales than the measured environmental gradients. At the scales resolved here, cockle
distribution in this well-connected fjord reflects the habitat larvae encounter more than spatial variation in how many arrive, although year-to-year variation in supply remains to be resolved.

\section{Introduction}

 
... Managing this fishery, and identifying where depleted beds might recover, requires understanding what determines where cockles occur and how much biomass they support.

The distribution of a benthic invertebrate with pelagic larvae depends on a sequence
of steps: larvae must be produced and transported to a site, settle there, and then
survive \citep{pinedaComplexitySimplificationUnderstanding2009}. Larval behaviour and small-scale physical processes can decouple the supply of larvae to a site from the number that settle there \citep{pinedaCausesDecouplingLarval2010}, and mortality in the first days and weeks after settlement is severe enough to reshape the pattern that settlement produced \citep{gosselinJuvenileMortalityBenthic1997,
huntRoleEarlyPostsettlement1997}. Correlative species distribution models (SDMs) address mainly the conditions a settler encounters once it has arrived \citep{robinsonPushingLimitsMarine2011}. By relating occurrence or biomass to environmental covariates, they implicitly assume that every environmentally suitable location is reachable, and therefore that propagule supply
does not limit distribution \citep{barveCrucialRoleAccessible2011}. For species whose dispersal capacity is largely confined to a pelagic larval stage lasting days to
weeks, this is a substantive ecological assumption rather than a technical one, yet it
is rarely tested explicitly.

The distinction carries direct management consequences. If larval supply limits occupancy, habitat-only models will predict suitable but unoccupied locations, overestimating the area available for recovery and potentially directing restoration toward sites that larvae seldom reach
\citep{griecoDesigningDispersalConnectivityinformed2026}. If instead larval supply is effectively ubiquitous within a system, habitat-only models are adequate, and effort
is better directed at habitat condition and at protecting spawning biomass than at enhancing supply to particular areas.

Larval supply is not uniform across a system. Transport is shaped by ocean circulation, larval traits and behaviour, and the spatial arrangement of suitable habitat, and can therefore generate substantial heterogeneity among locations in the number of larvae that arrive \citep{cowenLarvalDispersalMarine2009,
siegelStochasticNatureLarval2008}. Biophysical dispersal models represent this exchange as a connectivity network, in which habitat patches are nodes and simulated larval exchange forms links. Graph theory then provides node-level metrics summarising how much larval supply a patch receives and where it sits within that network, giving spatially explicit estimates of potential supply that can be used alongside
environmental predictors in an SDM. Such metrics have been used to identify source and sink areas and to inform conservation and restoration planning
\citep{hansenAssessingDemographicConnectivity2024,
griecoDesigningDispersalConnectivityinformed2026}, but how much they add beyond environmental predictors and spatial structure has rarely been assessed \citep[but see][]{cecinoTestingInfluenceSeascape2021}.

In the Limfjord specifically, \citet{hansenAssessingDemographicConnectivity2024}
used a biophysical model and graph-theoretic metrics to characterise cockle connectivity, showing that commercially productive beds depend largely on larval import from unexploited spawning biomass elsewhere rather than on self-recruitment, while other areas are comparatively isolated and rely on local retention. They further observed that some areas predicted to receive substantial larval input support no cockle populations, and suggested that environmental factors may be more limiting there than larval availability. Whether that is so has not been tested directly.

Testing this is challenging because dispersal and habitat are not independent. The same hydrodynamic processes shape both, and connectivity metrics are spatially smooth, so an apparent effect of connectivity can reflect environmental covariation or residual spatial structure rather than larval supply. 

Here we combine survey data for \textit{Cerastoderma} spp. in the Limfjorden (8{,}137 stations, 2018--2025) with a biophysical connectivity model to ask two questions. First, are cockles more likely to occur, and do they support more biomass, at sites receiving greater larval supply once habitat is accounted for? Second, can any such effect be distinguished from environmental gradients and residual spatial structure?

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Study system}

The Limfjord is a relatively large, shallow, semi-enclosed estuarine system crossing the Jutland Peninsula in northern Denmark, connecting the North Sea to the west with the Kattegat to the east (Fig.~\ref{fig:study_area}). It comprises a series of shallow basins linked by narrow straits and channels, a configuration that produces substantial spatial variation in water exchange and circulation \citep{hofmeisterThreedimensionalModelStudy2009}. Mean depth is approximately 4.5~m and the tidal range is small, around 0.2~m \citep{wilesStratificationMixingLimfjorden2006, dolmerEvaluationDanishMussel2002}. The system is eutrophic, with high nutrient loading from an agricultural catchment, and supports extensive benthic bivalve populations, including the cockle stocks considered here and a long-established blue mussel (\textit{Mytilus edulis}) fishery and aquaculture industry \citep{freitasSustainableCockleFishery2023, maarIntensifiedMusselFarming2023, dolmerEvaluationDanishMussel2002}. Summer deoxygenation events occur regularly and vary in extent across the system \citep{schourup-kristensenDriversHypoxiaVariability2023}.

Previous biophysical modelling indicates substantial larval transport among areas of the Limfjord despite this hydrographic heterogeneity. For blue mussels, simulations show larvae dispersing across hydrographically distinct parts of the system \citep{pastorAgentbasedModelingGenetics2021, pastorSensitivityAnalysisMussel2023}, and genetic evidence supports this, indicating that the fjord functions as a relatively well-connected system for that species \citep{pastorAgentbasedModelingGenetics2021}. For cockles, \citet{hansenAssessingDemographicConnectivity2024} found spatial variation in demographic connectivity within the fjord, with some beds depending on larval import from elsewhere and others on local retention.

\subsection{Connectivity metrics}

\textcolor{red}{@ Ane check if you like it}

The biophysical model simulated larval exchange among 2 $\times$ 2 km grid cells across the Limfjord. Its output was a matrix of larval counts settling between each pair of cells. We divided each count by the number of larvae released from its source cell, so that every edge gives the probability that a larva released in one cell settles in another.
    
We used presence-weighted in-strength as our primary connectivity metric (Fig.~\ref{fig:predictors}f). In-strength sums the weights of incoming edges to a cell \citep{squartiniReciprocityWeightedNetworks2013}, representing the potential larval supply received by that cell. Without weighting, all source cells contribute to this measure regardless of their likelihood of being occupied by cockles. We therefore weighted the outgoing edges of each source cell by its predicted probability of cockle presence, so that cells with a higher probability of occurrence contributed more strongly to potential larval supply. The resulting metric represents potential settlement supplied by likely occupied cells while accounting for spatial variation in predicted cockle occurrence.

The presence probability of each source cell was predicted from a Bernoulli GLMM fitted to the survey presence--absence data, containing only an intercept, survey and year as factors, and a spatial random field. The model was fitted using the same approach and barrier mesh as the species distribution models (Section~\ref{sec:sdm}). Predictions were made at the centre of every 2$\times$2~km grid cell containing at least one survey station, for the grab survey in the most recent year (2025). Cells without survey stations were assigned a weight of zero. We deliberately excluded environmental covariates from this model because they were already used to explain cockle distribution in the SDMs. Including them here would introduce the same environmental signal into the connectivity metric, potentially counting its contribution twice when connectivity and habitat suitability were considered jointly. Because the network weights are predicted occurrence probabilities and the occurrence component has presence as its response, a cell's own predicted occurrence must not contribute to its in-strength. We therefore excluded self-supply when computing connectivity metrics, so that each cell's value derives only from other cells.

For robustness, and to test whether our conclusions depended on how connectivity was summarised, we calculated four further graph metrics on the same presence-weighted network: in-degree, in-closeness, eigenvector centrality and transitivity (Supplementary Fig.~\rev{S3}). Whereas in-strength measures the amount of settlement a cell receives, these metrics describe the position of a cell within the dispersal network. In-degree is the number of source cells that send larvae to a given cell, including the cell itself when larvae are retained locally \citep{freemanCentralitySocialNetworks1978}. In-closeness is the inverse of the average shortest-path distance from cells that can reach a given cell \citep{estradaUSINGNETWORKCENTRALITY2008}. Edge probabilities $p$ were converted to distances as $-\log(p)$, such that the shortest path represents the most probable route through the network. Higher in-closeness therefore indicates cells that can be reached more readily through the network. Eigenvector centrality was calculated from the weighted, directed network, such that cells receiving larvae from highly central cells have greater centrality \citep{bonacichPowerCentralityFamily1987}. Transitivity is a graph metric that measures the proportion of possible triangular connections among the neighbours of a given node that are realised. It therefore quantifies how tightly connected the neighbourhood of a node is. Transitivity ranges from 0 to 1, with higher values indicating a greater proportion of realised triangular connections \citep{hansenAssessingDemographicConnectivity2024}.

All metrics were calculated in R using the \texttt{igraph} package \citep{csardi2006} following the graph construction of \citet{hansenAssessingDemographicConnectivity2024}. For every survey station we extracted each metric from the 2 × 2 km cell containing it.



\subsection{Species distribution models}
\label{sec:sdm}
We modelled cockle biomass density (g\,m$^{-2}$) using a spatial delta-gamma (hurdle) generalized linear mixed model (GLMM). Because cockles were absent from most sampling stations and biomass was strongly right-skewed when present, the model comprised two components: a binomial model for cockle occurrence and a Gamma model for positive biomass. For an observation $y_{\mathbf{s},t}$ at location $\mathbf{s}$ in year $t$ ($n = 8{,}137$),
\begin{align}
\Pr(y_{\mathbf{s},t} > 0) &= p_{\mathbf{s},t}, &
\operatorname{logit}(p_{\mathbf{s},t}) &= \eta_{1,\mathbf{s},t},\\
y_{\mathbf{s},t} \mid y_{\mathbf{s},t} > 0 &\sim \operatorname{Gamma}\!\left(\phi,\ \mu_{\mathbf{s},t}/\phi\right), &
\log(\mu_{\mathbf{s},t}) &= \eta_{2,\mathbf{s},t},
\end{align}
where $p_{\mathbf{s},t}$ is the probability of cockle occurrence, $\mu_{\mathbf{s},t}$ is the expected biomass conditional on occurrence, $\phi$ is the Gamma shape parameter, and  $\mu_{\mathbf{s},t}^2/\phi$ is the variance. Each component had its own linear predictor,
\begin{equation}
\eta_{m,\mathbf{s},t} =
\underbrace{\mathbf{X}_{\mathbf{s},t}\,\boldsymbol{\beta}_m}_{\text{survey, year}}
+ \underbrace{E_{m,\mathbf{s}}}_{\text{environment}}
+ \underbrace{C_{m,\mathbf{s}}}_{\text{connectivity}}
+ \underbrace{\omega_{m,\mathbf{s}}}_{\text{spatial random field}},
\end{equation}
where
\begin{align}
E_{m,\mathbf{s}} &= \sum_{k=1}^{6} \beta^{E}_{m,k}\, x_{k,\mathbf{s}},
&& \mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{s}} = (\text{depth},\ \text{depth}^2,\ \text{temperature},\ \text{oxygen},\ \text{salinity},\ \text{shear stress})_{\mathbf{s}},\\
C_{m,\mathbf{s}} &= \beta^{C}_m\, c_{\mathbf{s}},
&& c_{\mathbf{s}} = \text{connectivity metric},\\
\boldsymbol{\omega}_m &\sim \operatorname{MVN}\!\left(\mathbf{0}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\omega,m}\right).
\end{align}
Here $m \in \{1, 2\}$ denotes the occurrence and positive-biomass components, respectively. The model matrix $\mathbf{X}_{\mathbf{s},t}$ contains an intercept and survey and year as categorical effects, with coefficients $\boldsymbol{\beta}_m$. Survey was included to account for differences in catch efficiency among the three gears, while year accounted for interannual differences in cockle occurrence and biomass. $E_{m,\mathbf{s}}$ is the effect of the five environmental predictors (Fig.~\ref{fig:predictors}a--e), with depth entering as a quadratic, and $C_{m,\mathbf{s}}$ is the effect of the connectivity value $c_{\mathbf{s}}$ of the 2~$\times$~2~km grid cell containing the sampling station (Fig.~\ref{fig:predictors}f). $\omega_{m,\mathbf{s}}$ is a spatially structured Gaussian random field with Matérn covariance, with spatial dependence characterized by a range parameter and marginal variance $\sigma^2_{\omega,m}$. The two components had separate fixed effects and spatial random fields, allowing the environmental and connectivity effects on cockle occurrence to differ from those on biomass conditional on occurrence. Continuous predictors were standardized to zero mean and unit variance before model fitting. We calculated pairwise Spearman correlations among all predictors, including connectivity metrics (Fig.~S{X}). Correlations among the five environmental predictors were all below $|\rho| = 0.7$.

Predictions of expected cockle biomass were obtained by multiplying the predicted probability of occurrence by the predicted positive biomass. Prediction uncertainty was propagated by drawing from the joint parameter covariance matrix and calculating expected biomass for each draw.

\subsubsection{Spatial mesh}

Survey effort was uneven, with a few cockle beds sampled densely by grab in most years, while the rest of the fjord was covered more sparsely by dredge surveys (2018 suction dredge and KSKV). We therefore built a non-uniform mesh, with smaller triangles where sampling was dense and larger triangles where it was sparse. This keeps the SPDE approximation accurate where the data can resolve fine-scale variation, without wasting vertices elsewhere \citep{lindgren2018}. Because the Limfjord is a narrow, branching fjord we used a barrier model \citep{bakkaNonstationaryGaussianModels2019}, setting the correlation range in mesh triangles over land to 10\% of the range in water. This forces spatial correlation to follow the water and decay rapidly across land (Supplementary Fig.~\rev{S4}).


\subsubsection{Model comparison}

To quantify the contribution of environmental conditions and larval connectivity beyond residual spatial structure, we compared four model structures (Table~\ref{tab:model_structures}), all including the spatial random field ($\omega$), with environment ($E$) and connectivity ($C$) included in different combinations.

The same set of predictors was included in both model components (occurrence and positive biomass). We first compared competing models using Akaike’s Information Criterion (AIC; \citet{akaike1974}), which evaluates relative in-sample model support while accounting for model complexity, with lower AIC values indicating greater support. Because the two components share no parameters, the delta model's likelihood is the sum of the likelihoods of a binomial model fitted to occurrence at all stations and a Gamma model fitted to biomass at the stations where cockles were present. We therefore also fitted the two components separately and compared models for occurrence and for positive biomass, as well as for both together (the total AIC, the sum of the two).

To assess whether environmental conditions and connectivity improved predictions at locations not used for model fitting, we additionally compared the four model structures using spatially blocked 10-fold cross-validation \citep{roberts2017crossvalidation}. The spatial blocks were the 2~$\times$2km grid cells of the connectivity model, such that all stations within a cell were held out together, and cells were randomly assigned to ten folds, which were kept identical across models (Supplementary Fig.~\rev{S5}). Each model was fitted ten times, with one fold omitted in each iteration, and predictions were generated for the held-out observations. Prediction uncertainty was incorporated by sampling 500 times from the multivariate normal distribution defined by the maximum-likelihood estimates and their estimated covariance matrix. Models were compared using the expected log predictive density (ELPD) of the held-out observations \citep{vehtari2017practical}. The two components were cross-validated separately. Because cockles occurred in only a subset of cells, the positive-biomass component had its own folds, formed in the same way from the cells containing stations with cockles, so that each fold held out a similar number of occupied cells. The total ELPD of each station was the sum of its occurrence term and, for stations with cockles, its positive-biomass term. For each predictor block (environment or connectivity), we calculated the difference in ELPD between models with and without that block, together with its standard error across observations. Positive ELPD differences indicate improved out-of-sample predictive performance.

As a sensitivity analysis, we refitted the full model (Space + Environment + Connectivity) separately with each of four alternative connectivity metrics, replacing in-strength with the corresponding metric while keeping the remaining model specification unchanged, and compared the resulting models using AIC.

\subsubsection{Software}
We fit the spatial models with the R package \texttt{sdmTMB} \citep{andersonSdmTMBPackageFast2025}, development version 1.1.0.9024. sdmTMB uses automatic differentiation and the Laplace approximation from the R package TMB \citep{kristensenTMBAutomaticDifferentiation2016} and sparse matrix structures to set up the SPDE-based GMRFs from the R package \texttt{fmesher} \citep{lindgrenFmesherTriangleMeshes2025}. Estimation is done via maximum marginal likelihood. We confirmed that the nonlinear optimization was consistent with convergence by checking that the Hessian matrix was positive definite and the maximum absolute log-likelihood gradient with respect to fixed effects was <0.001 \citep{andersonSdmTMBPackageFast2025}. \rev{We assessed model fit with simulation-based residuals: we simulated 500 biomass values at each station from the fitted model, drawing parameters from the multivariate normal distribution of the maximum-likelihood estimates, and computed each observation's quantile among its simulations with the R package \texttt{DHARMa} \citep{hartigDHARMaResidualDiagnostics2022}. We checked these residuals for uniformity with a Q–Q plot, mapped them by station (Supplementary Fig.~S6), and compared the observed and simulated proportions of zeros.} ELPD was calculated with the R package \texttt{loo} \citep{vehtari2017practical}. All analyses were done in R version 4.6.1 \citep{rcore2024}.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Cockles occur in a few dense beds, although larvae reach the whole fjord}

Cockles were present at 1{,}527 of 8{,}137 stations (18.8\%), and biomass was concentrated in a few dense beds along the central channel and in the western and southern basins (Supplementary Fig.~\rev{S7}). This patchiness was not matched by larval supply: simulated larvae dispersed widely from every release cell (Fig.~\ref{fig:study_area}b), and every survey station received some larval input (presence-weighted in-strength $>0$ at all stations). Larvae therefore reached areas both with and without established beds.

\subsection{Habitat determines where cockles occur}

Occurrence was strongly structured by the environment (Table~\ref{tab:model-selection}).
Adding the environmental predictors to the spatial field lowered AIC by 61 and improved prediction of occurrence in held-out grid cells ($\Delta$ELPD $=68.4$,
SE $10.4$). The probability of presence was highest at intermediate depths, peaking at 7.1~m (95\% CI 5.9--8.0~m), increased with salinity ($0.82$ per SD; 95\% CI $0.43$ to $1.21$) and declined with
temperature ($-0.51$; $-0.89$ to $-0.13$) and maximum shear stress
($-0.68$; $-0.99$ to $-0.36$) (Fig.~\ref{fig:coefficients}b). Residual spatial autocorrelation decayed over a Mat\'ern range of 2.2~km, so the structure remaining after accounting for the environment was fine-scale relative to the
extent of the fjord.

\subsection{Larval supply adds little to occurrence, and is highest where habitat is poor}

Larval connectivity made at most a small and inconsistent contribution to
explaining occurrence. Adding in-strength to the model containing environment and space lowered AIC by only 1.8, but improved prediction in held-out cells
($\Delta$ELPD $=18.8$, SE $4.6$; Table~\ref{tab:model-selection}). Without the environmental predictors, however, in-strength did not improve prediction ($\Delta$ELPD $=-6.6$, SE $3.4$), indicating that it carries predictive information only in combination with habitat covariates rather than in its own right.

The direction of the effect was opposite to a larval-subsidy expectation: the probability of presence tended to decrease with in-strength ($-0.27$, 95\% CI $-0.55$ to $0.00$; Fig.~\ref{fig:coefficients}). This is consistent with where modelled larvae accumulate. In-strength was highest in deeper ($\rho=0.50$), less
saline ($\rho=-0.55$) and more frequently hypoxic ($\rho=0.37$) areas
(Supplementary Fig.~\rev{S2}) --- that is, in areas our environmental model identifies as poor cockle habitat.

Results were similar across connectivity metrics. In-strength, chosen \emph{a priori}, also gave the lowest AIC ($\Delta$AIC $+2.7$ to $+3.8$ for the four alternatives; Supplementary Table~S1), but no metric had a coefficient whose confidence interval excluded zero (Supplementary Fig.~\rev{S8}), so this ranking distinguishes among uniformly weak effects.

\subsection{Neither habitat nor larval supply explains biomass among beds}

Where cockles were present, their biomass was not better explained by the environment or by connectivity than by the spatial field alone (Table~\ref{tab:model-selection}). The Space model had the lowest AIC for positive biomass, and adding the environment ($\Delta$AIC $=+1.6$), in-strength ($+1.9$) or both ($+3.5$) did not improve it. The Space model also gave the best predictions of biomass in held-out grid cells (Table~\ref{tab:model-selection}). 

Two environmental coefficients were distinguishable from zero --- biomass
declined with maximum shear stress ($-0.31$, 95\% CI $-0.61$ to $-0.01$) and with oxygen ($-0.59$, $-1.14$ to $-0.05$) --- but adding the environmental predictors as a set did not improve fit or prediction, suggesting that the variation they
describe can be largely absorbed by the spatial random field. The in-strength coefficient was close to zero ($-0.04$, $-0.27$ to $0.20$; Fig.~\ref{fig:coefficients}), and no
alternative connectivity metric improved the fit ($\Delta$AIC within 1 of in-strength; Supplementary Table~S1). 


Combining the two components, the model with space and environment and the model that additionally included in-strength were indistinguishable ($\Delta$AIC $=0.1$; Table~\ref{tab:model-selection}). Once habitat and spatial structure were accounted for, connectivity therefore added essentially nothing to the joint explanation of where cockles occurred and how much biomass they supported --- although, as above, cross-validation indicated a modest predictive contribution for occurrence that this comparison does not capture.

\section{Discussion}
Simulated larvae reached every one of the 8{,}137 surveyed stations in the Limfjord, yet cockles occurred at fewer than one in five. Occupancy was strongly related to the physical environment, whereas adding modelled connectivity to a model already containing environmental predictors and spatial structure improved predictive performance little. Among occupied beds, biomass was not explained by either habitat or connectivity, but by fine-scale spatial structure. Together, these results suggest that, at the spatial and temporal scales resolved here, larval supply does not constrain the distribution of cockles across the Limfjord. Instead, the principal spatial filter appears to occur after larvae have the opportunity to arrive, through the environmental conditions experienced at potential habitat.

This interpretation requires an important distinction between larval supply, settlement and recruitment. We simulated the first of these. The arrival of simulated larvae in a grid cell indicates that larvae could reach that location, but does not establish that they settled or survived after settlement. Larval behaviour and small-scale hydrodynamic processes can decouple dispersal from settlement \citep{pinedaCausesDecouplingLarval2010}, while post-settlement mortality can exceed 90\% in benthic invertebrates and operate over small spatial scales \citep{gosselinJuvenileMortalityBenthic1997, huntRoleEarlyPostsettlement1997}. A useful example comes from \textit{Ostrea lurida}, for which inner-estuarine sites received abundant larvae but produced few recruits because low salinity caused high post-settlement mortality, whereas outer sites were more supply-limited \citep{pritchardVariationLarvalSupply2016}. The same distinction may apply here: our results cannot determine whether larvae fail to settle in unsuitable parts of the Limfjord or whether they settle but subsequently die. Either mechanism would produce widespread larval supply alongside restricted occurrence.

The weak contribution of connectivity is therefore not evidence that dispersal is ecologically unimportant. Rather, it suggests that variation in larval supply does not explain much of the remaining spatial variation in cockle occupancy once habitat and spatial structure are accounted for. A similar pattern has been reported for other marine species. \citet{cecinoTestingInfluenceSeascape2021} incorporated network centrality metrics alongside environmental variables when modelling the distributions of a sea urchin and a snapper. Connectivity did not reduce predictive performance, but its contribution was substantially smaller than that of environmental predictors. Such results are consistent with the idea that connectivity may be permissive rather than limiting: larvae may need to reach suitable habitat, but once dispersal is sufficiently widespread, further variation in supply contributes little to explaining where adults occur.

The negative association between in-strength and habitat suitability provides additional support for this interpretation. In-strength was highest in deeper, less saline and more frequently hypoxic areas ($\rho = 0.50$, $-0.55$ and $0.37$, respectively), which were also among the least suitable areas according to our habitat model. Thus, areas receiving greater potential larval supply did not necessarily provide more suitable conditions for cockle persistence, indicating a spatial decoupling between connectivity and habitat suitability. Such decoupling is expected when the physical processes that transport and concentrate larvae differ from those determining post-settlement survival and population persistence \citep[e.g.][]{whiteScaleDependentChangesImportance2008}. This pattern was anticipated in the Limfjord by \citet{hansenAssessingDemographicConnectivity2024}, who found high larval supply in areas where cockles were absent and suggested that environmental conditions may impose stronger constraints on population establishment than larval availability. Consequently, high larval supply may occur in locations where subsequent environmental filtering limits population establishment, survival, or persistence.

The spatial structure of biomass points to an additional demographic filter operating after occupancy has been established. A spatial random field provided the strongest explanation of biomass among occupied beds, with a spatial range of 2.7km. This is approximately the scale of cockle patchiness reported from comparable surveys \citep{kraanPatchinessMacrobenthicInvertebrates2009}. The spatial field likely captures a combination of processes not represented by our covariates, including finer-scale sediment and habitat structure, the location and persistence of established beds, and local disturbance. Fishing is one possible contributor: physical disturbance can modify sediment and potentially reduce settlement, although the magnitude of these effects remain debated \citep{piersmaLongtermIndirectEffects2001, vandermeerReevaluationEffectsMechanical2023}. More fundamentally, standing biomass integrates demographic history. A bed’s current biomass may reflect previous recruitment events and cohort survival more strongly than present-day environmental conditions. For example, \citet{magalhaesCocklePopulationDynamics2016} found that recruitment timing determined cohort density, lifespan and production in an unexploited cockle population, whereas adult spawner biomass did not predict subsequent recruitment. Similarly, a single cohort of approximately 60{,}000 spat~m$^{-2}$ suppressed subsequent recruitment in a Danish population before the population eventually disappeared \citep{f46a34c986624a1baee20a526e50e3c9}. Where a small number of strong year classes dominate standing stock, spatial variation in present-day habitat may therefore explain little of the variation in biomass.

The estimated habitat relationships are nevertheless broadly consistent with the known ecology of cockles. The positive effect of salinity is consistent with experimental evidence that newly settled juveniles experience high mortality below a salinity of approximately 15 psu \citep{peteiroResponsesSalinityStress2018}, as well as with the separation of \textit{Cerastoderma} species along the salinity gradient of the Baltic and its transition zone \citep{brockGeographicalDistributionCerastoderma1980}. The negative temperature relationship may have both direct and indirect components. Mean bottom temperature can covary with summer stratification and stagnation \citep{schourup-kristensenDriversHypoxiaVariability2023}, and may therefore partly represent the same environmental processes captured by oxygen. Oxygen itself may operate primarily through chronic rather than acute stress: cockles can tolerate concentrations considerably below our 4 mg~l$^{-1}$ threshold for days to weeks \citep{rosenbergHypoxicToleranceMarine1991}. Persistent exposure could therefore reduce growth and condition without causing immediate mortality, affecting the biomass carried by established beds more strongly than the probability of occurrence. This interpretation is consistent with oxygen contributing to the biomass component but not the occurrence component of our model.

Some limitations constrain the strength and scale of these conclusions. First, sampling effort was highly uneven by design. A small number of beds were sampled intensively with grabs, whereas dredge surveys covered much of the remaining fjord more sparsely, and the two gears differ substantially in catch efficiency, with biomass estimates from dredges being up to approximately 80 times lower. Because standardised sampling across the entire fjord was unavailable for this period, we accommodated rather than removed this structure by including survey as a factor in all models and refining the mesh in densely sampled areas. Nevertheless, residual differences in detectability or sampling design could contribute to the spatial structure attributed to the model.

Second, the spatial resolution of the connectivity estimates limits the scale at which connectivity effects can be detected. Connectivity was calculated on 2~$\times$2~km cells, approximately matching the scale of the residual spatial fields. Connectivity effects operating at substantially finer scales would therefore be averaged out. In addition, the connectivity estimates inherit the spatial resolution and assumptions of the underlying hydrodynamic model, which can influence simulated dispersal as much as biological parameterisation \citep{sciasciaLarvalDispersalSimulations2022,saint-amandBiophysicalModelsResolution2023}. Our results should consequently be interpreted as evidence against a strong broad-scale connectivity signal at the resolution considered, rather than against connectivity effects in general.

Third, both connectivity and environmental predictors were summarised as 2010–2016 means, while survey years were represented by factor effects. The analysis therefore addresses the spatial association between average larval supply and average habitat conditions over the survey period, rather than whether year-specific larval supply predicts subsequent recruitment. A time-varying connectivity model could test whether annual mismatches between larval supply and environmental conditions generate recruitment responses, although the relatively short survey series currently limits the information available for such an analysis. Similarly, our connectivity metric describes potential larval supply rather than realised settlement, and thus cannot distinguish dispersal limitation from mortality or other post-settlement processes.

Spatial confounding is another consideration when interpreting the relative contribution of connectivity and the spatial field. A spatially structured covariate and a spatial random field can compete to explain the same variation, potentially biasing covariate estimates and reallocating variation between model components \citep{huiSpatialConfoundingJoint2024,makinenSpatialConfoundingBayesian2022}. The extent of this competition depends on the spatial scales of the processes involved \citep{paciorekImportanceScaleSpatialConfounding2010}. In our case, the fitted spatial fields had short ranges and therefore primarily absorbed fine-scale residual variation, whereas connectivity and the environmental predictors varied more smoothly across the fjord. This separation of scales should reduce, although not eliminate, competition between the connectivity covariate and the residual field. Importantly, the negative relationship between in-strength and habitat suitability is also inconsistent with a simple interpretation in which the spatial field merely absorbs a positive connectivity effect. Nevertheless, the possibility of scale-dependent confounding means that the absence of a strong connectivity signal should not be interpreted as definitive evidence that connectivity has no effect.

From a management perspective, these results suggest that improving habitat conditions may be more effective than targeting larval source areas when larval supply is already widespread. Simulated larvae reached essentially the entire survey domain, meaning that increasing spawning biomass in particular source areas is unlikely, by itself, to expand the area available to cockles if larvae already reach those locations. Protecting established beds from physical disturbance and chronic deoxygenation may therefore be more direct management levers. This conclusion is conditional on larval supply being sufficiently widespread. Where supply is limiting, connectivity can become much more important. For example, \citet{mcgeadyLongtermInterannualVariability2022} found that year-specific larval dispersal indices predicted adult density in the Norway lobster (\textit{Nephrops norvegicus}) grounds with consistently low larval supply, but not in grounds where supply was consistently high. Connectivity-informed management is therefore most compelling where dispersal actually constrains population establishment \citep{griecoDesigningDispersalConnectivityinformed2026}.

The importance of connectivity should therefore depend on whether larvae limit access to suitable habitat. Where dispersal is pervasive, larval supply becomes similar among sites and little spatial variation remains for connectivity to explain, whereas isolated or weakly connected systems can remain strongly structured by dispersal limitation
\citep{heinoMetacommunityOrganisationSpatial2015}. Connectivity can also matter for a different reason, by limiting whether populations reach suitable habitat they do not yet occupy. \citet{assisOceanographicConnectivityStrongly2026} projected future ranges of marine forest species from environmental suitability and then removed the suitable areas that modelled larvae could not reach, which substantially reduced projected expansion. Connectivity there constrained access
to new habitat rather than competing with habitat inside the occupied range.

The Limfjord lies nearer the other end of this gradient. Simulated larvae
reach essentially the whole surveyed area, and environmental conditions restrict where cockles occur within it. Assuming that larvae can access suitable habitat may therefore be reasonable here, but it remains an assumption, and one worth testing in systems where dispersal is more restricted \citep{barveCrucialRoleAccessible2011}. Widespread larval dispersal need not produce widespread adult populations. For cockles in the Limfjord, the conditions larvae encounter on arrival seem to matter more than variation in how many arrive.
