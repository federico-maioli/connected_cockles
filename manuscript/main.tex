% ---- ABSTRACT --------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Abstract}
% ~150--300 words. State the question, the data/approach, the key findings,
% and why they matter.
Correlative species distribution models for sessile marine invertebrates generally assume unrestricted larval access to all suitable habitat, yet propagule supply is computable from biophysical transport models and is rarely tested as a predictor. We combined a X station survey of the common cockle \textit{Cerastoderma edule} biomass in the Limfjorden, Denmark, with a particle-tracking simulation of larval dispersal to ask whether connectivity metrics improve a habitat-based spatial GLMM of cockle standing stock. Environmental predictors — depth, salinity, temperature, oxygen — explained [X]\% of the deviance, while connectivity metrics added [Y] percentage points and had mostly negative coefficients. Independent genetic and agent-based-modelling evidence that the Limfjorden is a single well-connected unit supports a species-sorting interpretation in which post-settlement processes, not larval delivery, set adult distribution. Rather than a case against connectivity in distribution models, we present two computable diagnostics — the among-site spread of modelled supply and its collinearity with environmental predictors — that indicate in advance whether a connectivity covariate can inform a distribution model in a given system

Larval dispersal was simulated with a biophysical individual-based model that
couples a hydrodynamic ocean model with Lagrangian particle tracking and a
larval pelagic-duration/behaviour module \citep{yourABMref}. The study area was
discretized into 2x2 km grid cells. Virtual larvae were released from each cell and tracked until settlement; aggregating over all release events yielded a connectivity matrix $C$, where $C_{jk}$ is the number of larvae released in cell $j$ that settled in cell $k$, together with a release vector $R_j$.

We converted these into a flow matrix $F$ of settlement probabilities,
\begin{equation}
F_{jk} = \frac{C_{jk}}{R_j}, \qquad 0 \le \sum_{k} F_{jk} \le 1,
\end{equation}
so that $F_{jk}$ is the probability that a larva released in source cell $j$
settles in sink cell $k$ (rows index sources, columns sinks). We treated $F$ as
the weighted adjacency matrix of a directed dispersal network.

eigenvector centrality,

\subsection{Species distribution models}

We related cockle occurrence and biomass to environmental conditions, sampling gear, and larval connectivity using spatial generalized linear mixed models fitted with the \texttt{sdmTMB} package \citep{anderson2024} in \textsf{R}.For each survey location $i$ we modelled two responses: the presence--absence indicator $z_i\in\{0,1\}$ and the biomass density $y_i\ge 0$ (g\,m$^{-2}$).

Presence was modelled with a Bernoulli distribution and a logit link,
\begin{equation}
z_i \sim \mathrm{Bernoulli}(\pi_i), \qquad \operatorname{logit}(\pi_i) = \eta_i ,
\end{equation}
and biomass with a Tweedie distribution (compound Poisson--gamma, power
parameter $1<p<2$) and a log link,
\begin{equation}
y_i \sim \operatorname{Tweedie}(\mu_i,\phi,p), \qquad \log(\mu_i) = \eta_i .
\end{equation}

Both responses shared the same linear predictor,
\begin{equation}
\eta_i = \beta_0 + \gamma_{g(i)} + \sum_{k=1}^{5}\beta_k\,x_{ik} + \theta\,c_i + \omega_{s_i},
\end{equation}
where $\gamma_{g(i)}$ is the gear effect, $x_{ik}$ are the five (standardized)
environmental covariates, $c_i$ is connectivity, and $\omega_{s_i}$ is a spatial
random field.
