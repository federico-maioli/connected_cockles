% ---- ABSTRACT --------------------------------------------------------------
\section*{Abstract}
Correlative species distribution models (SDMs) for marine invertebrates with pelagic larvae typically rely on environmental predictors, implicitly assuming that larvae reach all suitable habitat. Yet larval supply varies in space, and whether it adds to habitat in explaining occurrence and abundance has rarely been tested. We quantified the contributions of habitat, larval supply and spatial structure to the occurrence and biomass of cockles (\textit{Cerastoderma} spp.) in the Limfjorden, Denmark. We combined 8{,}378 stations from three surveys (2018--2025) with environmental predictors from a coupled hydrodynamic--biogeochemical model and connectivity metrics from a biophysical larval dispersal model. We compared two-part spatial SDMs with and without environmental and connectivity predictors, accounting for differences in detection among survey gears, using AIC and spatially blocked cross-validation. Simulated larvae reached nearly every surveyed station, yet cockles occurred at only 18\% of them. Occurrence was structured by habitat: it peaked at about 7~m depth, followed the salinity--temperature gradient along the fjord, and declined with bottom shear stress. Predicted larval supply modestly improved predictions of occurrence, but cockles were less likely to occur where supply was highest, which was largely in poorer habitat. Among occupied beds, biomass varied among years and was lower after fishing in the preceding season and where low oxygen was frequent, with only a weak association with larval supply. At the scale of the fjord, larval supply therefore did not limit cockle distribution, and habitat-based SDMs captured most of the explainable variation. Whether larval supply matters at finer spatial and temporal scales remains to be tested.

% ---- INTRODUCTION ----------------------------------------------------------
\section{Introduction}

Cockles (\textit{Cerastoderma} spp.) are among the most abundant and most heavily exploited bivalves of European estuaries and coastal lagoons
\citep{malhamReviewBiologyEuropean2012}. They filter organic matter from the water column, rework and stabilise surface sediments, and are prey for shorebirds, crabs and demersal fish \citep{carssEcosystemServicesProvided2020}. Commercial cockle fisheries operate across much of Europe, and almost all take cockles from wild beds
rather than from culture \citep{carssEcosystemServicesProvided2020}. In the
Limfjorden, Denmark, a cockle fishery has developed only in the last decade, and unlike almost all European cockle fisheries, which work intertidal flats, it operates in sublittoral waters deeper than 3~m \citep{Freitas2024}. Managing this fishery, and identifying where depleted cockle beds might recover, requires understanding what determines where cockles occur and how much biomass they support.

The distribution of a benthic invertebrate with pelagic larvae depends on a sequence of steps. Larvae must be produced, survive transport to a site, settle there, and then survive after settlement \citep{pinedaComplexitySimplificationUnderstanding2009}.
Environmental conditions during the pelagic phase affect larval growth and survival, and therefore how many larvae reach a site \citep{cowenLarvalDispersalMarine2009}. Larval behaviour and small-scale physical processes can then decouple larval supply from settlement \citep{pinedaCausesDecouplingLarval2010}. If post-settlement mortality is high, the distribution of recruits may in turn depart from the settlement pattern \citep{gosselinJuvenileMortalityBenthic1997,
huntRoleEarlyPostsettlement1997}. 

Correlative species distribution models (SDMs) address mainly the conditions at the sites where larvae arrive \citep{robinsonPushingLimitsMarine2011}. By relating occurrence or biomass to environmental covariates, they implicitly assume that every environmentally suitable location is reachable, and therefore that propagule supply does not limit distribution \citep{barveCrucialRoleAccessible2011}. For species whose dispersal capacity is largely confined to a pelagic larval stage lasting days to weeks, this is a substantive ecological assumption rather than a technical one, yet it is rarely tested explicitly.

The distinction carries direct management consequences. If larval supply limits occupancy, habitat-only models will predict suitable but unoccupied locations, overestimating the area available for recovery and potentially directing restoration toward sites that larvae seldom reach
\citep{griecoDesigningDispersalConnectivityinformed2026}. If instead larval supply is effectively ubiquitous within a system, habitat-only models are adequate, and effort is better directed at habitat condition and at protecting overall spawning biomass than at enhancing supply to particular areas.

Larval supply is not uniform across a system. Transport is shaped by ocean circulation, larval traits and behaviour, and the spatial arrangement of suitable habitat, and can therefore generate substantial heterogeneity among locations in the number of larvae that arrive \citep{cowenLarvalDispersalMarine2009,
siegelStochasticNatureLarval2008}. Biophysical dispersal models simulate this transport by tracking virtual larvae through a modelled flow filed, and the resulting exchange among habitat patches can be represented as a network, with patches as nodes and simulated transfer between them as links. Graph theory then provides node-level metrics summarising how much larval supply a patch is predicted to receive and where it sits within that network. Such metrics describe potential supply rather than realised settlement or recruitment, but they give spatially explicit predictors that can be used alongside environmental predictors in an SDM. They have been used to identify source and sink areas and to inform conservation and restoration planning
\citep{hansenAssessingDemographicConnectivity2024,
griecoDesigningDispersalConnectivityinformed2026}, but how much they add beyond environmental predictors and spatial structure has rarely been assessed \citep[but see][]{cecinoTestingInfluenceSeascape2021}.

In the Limfjord specifically, \citet{hansenAssessingDemographicConnectivity2024} used a biophysical model and graph-theoretic metrics to characterise cockle connectivity. They found that commercially productive beds depend largely on larval
import from unexploited spawning stock elsewhere, while other areas are comparatively isolated and rely mostly on self-recruitment. They also observed that some areas predicted to receive substantial larval input support no cockle populations, and suggested that environmental factors may be more limiting there than larval availability. Whether that is so has not been tested directly.

Testing this is challenging because dispersal and habitat are not independent. The same hydrodynamic processes shape both, and connectivity metrics are spatially smooth, so an apparent effect of connectivity can reflect environmental covariation or residual spatial structure rather than larval supply. Assessing whether connectivity
adds explanatory power therefore requires accounting for environmental predictors and spatial structure at the same time.

Here we combine survey data for \textit{Cerastoderma} spp. in the Limfjord (8{,}137 stations, 2018--2025) with environmental predictors from a coupled hydrodynamic and biogeochemical model and connectivity metrics from a biophysical dispersal model. We test whether cockles are more likely to occur, and support more biomass, at sites receiving greater larval supply once habitat is accounted for, and whether any such effect can be distinguished from environmental gradients and residual spatial structure.


% ---- METHODS ---------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Methods}

\subsection{Study system}

Limfjorden is a relatively large, shallow, semi-enclosed estuarine system crossing the Jutland Peninsula in northern Denmark, connecting the North Sea to the west with the Kattegat to the east (Fig.~\ref{fig:study_area}). It comprises a series of shallow basins linked by narrow straits and channels, a configuration that produces substantial spatial variation in water exchange and circulation \citep{hofmeisterThreedimensionalModelStudy2009}. Mean depth is approximately 4.5~m and the tidal range is small, around 0.2~m \citep{wilesStratificationMixingLimfjorden2006, dolmerEvaluationDanishMussel2002}. Limfjorden is an eutrophic system, with high nutrient loading from an agricultural catchment, and summer oxygen depletion events occur regularly with variable extent across the system \citep{schourup-kristensenDriversHypoxiaVariability2023}. 

Until recently, large populations of blue mussel \textit{Mytilus edulis}, European flat oyster \textit{Ostrea edulis} and cockles (\textit{Cerastoderma} spp.) supported significant commercial fisheries in the Limfjorden \citep[e.g.][]{nielsenFlatOysterFishery2019, nielsenManagementBivalveFisheries2021,
Freitas2024}. The cockle fishery was the most valuable bivalve
fishery in Denmark and the largest cockle fishery in Europe, accounting for 40\% of Danish bivalve landings by value (2017--2023; \citealp{fiskeristyrelsen2026}) and 58\% of European cockle landings by weight (2017--2023; \citealp{eurostat2026}).

Previous biophysical modelling indicates substantial larval transport among areas of the Limfjorden despite this hydrographic heterogeneity. For blue mussels, simulations show larvae dispersing across hydrographically distinct parts of the system \citep{pastorAgentbasedModelingGenetics2021, pastorSensitivityAnalysisMussel2023}, and genetic evidence supports this, indicating that the fjord functions as a relatively well-connected system for that species \citep{pastorAgentbasedModelingGenetics2021}. For cockles, \citet{hansenAssessingDemographicConnectivity2024} found spatial variation in demographic connectivity within the fjord, with some beds depending on larval import from elsewhere and others on local retention.

\subsection{Cockles}
The common cockle \textit{Cerastoderma edule} and the lagoon cockle
\textit{C.~glaucum} are infaunal bivalves that live buried just below the sediment surface in sandy to muddy habitats across the northeastern Atlantic, from Iceland and Norway to Senegal and the Black Sea \citep{Dabouineau2011,
malhamReviewBiologyEuropean2012}. Cockles (\textit{Cerastoderma} spp.) are of
economic and cultural importance \citep[e.g.][]{carssEcosystemServicesProvided2020}, and they modify the sediments they inhabit, acting as ecosystem engineers \citep[e.g.][]{donadiMultiscaleHabitatModification2015}. Both species occur in the
Limfjorden, often in sympatric populations \citep{brockGeographicalDistributionCerastoderma1980,
ivellQUANTITATIVESTUDYCERASTODERMA1981, Freitas2024}, and can be distinguished
morphologically \citep{Parada2018}.

\subsection{Cockle surveys}\label{sec:surveys}

We combined cockle data from three surveys that differ in gear, sampled area and spatial coverage (Fig.~\ref{fig:study_area}a). The 2018 cockle stock survey used a hydraulic suction dredge, 0.75 m wide, towed over 44--392 m (swept area 33--294 m$^2$, median 115 m$^2$), and covered the fjord on a sparse grid of stations \citep{freitasSustainableCockleFishery2023} ($n = 283$; Supplementary Fig.~S1). The 2021--2025 cockle stock surveys used a 0.1~m$^2$ Day grab, digging about 15 cm into the sediment, to sample selected beds and fishing areas at high resolution, and therefore covered variable and limited parts of the Limfjord \citep{Freitas2021, Freitas2022, freitasSustainableCockleFishery2023, Freitas2023b,
Freitas2024b, Freitas2025} (n = 5{,}305). We further added cockle catches from the 2018--2025 blue mussel and oyster surveys (Konsekvensvurdering, KSKV;
\citealp{nielsenFlatOysterFishery2019}), which use a 0.965 m wide oyster dredge towed over roughly 100 or 400 m (dredged area 81--670~m$^2$, median 375~m$^2$), retaining only stations within 4~km of a
cockle survey station so that the spatial domain remained that of the cockle surveys (n = 2{,}790). In total the data comprised 8{,}378 stations, of which 1{,}537 (18.3\%) held cockles (biomass > 0).

The three surveys differ in what they can tell us about cockles. The grab samples a small, fixed area with full efficiency and gives reliable biomass. The suction dredge also samples with full efficiency, but over an area roughly a thousand times larger, so its positive samples mostly record sparse cockles at densities a grab cannot register (a single cockle in a grab corresponds to 10 individuals~m$^{-2}$): the median positive biomass was 3.9 g~m$^{-2}$ in the suction-dredge survey and 2{,}630 g~m$^{-2}$ in the grab surveys. Its positive biomass is therefore not comparable with that of the grab. The oyster dredge catches cockles with variable and unknown efficiency, so its records confirm presence but do not reliably establish absence or quantify biomass \citep{Freitas2024}. We therefore used all three surveys to model occurrence, with survey-specific detection, and only the grab survey to model biomass where present (Section \ref{sec:sdm}).

\subsection{Environmental and fishing predictors}\label{sec:env}


Our environmental predictors were depth, bottom temperature, bottom salinity, the number of days per year with bottom dissolved oxygen below 4~mg~l$^{-1}$ (hereafter oxygen), the maximum bottom shear stress from waves and currents, and sediment type (Fig.~\ref{fig:predictors}a--f). We also included the intensity of the cockle fishery (Fig.~\ref{fig:predictors}g). Sediment type entered the models as a factor and all other predictors as continuous variables.

Temperature, salinity and oxygen can constrain cockle survival and performance. Temperature and oxygen depletion have both been associated with cockle mortality \citep{burdonMassMortalitiesBivalve2014}, and salinity determines the distribution of the two \textit{Cerastoderma} species across the Baltic and adjacent seas \citep{brockGeographicalDistributionCerastoderma1980}. Depth and shear stress represent the bathymetric and hydrodynamic conditions of the seabed, and sediment type describes the substrate that cockles burrow into \citep{malhamReviewBiologyEuropean2012}. We included fishing intensity because dredging removes cockle biomass directly and disturbs the surface sediment, so it can depress biomass at a site independently of habitat conditions.

Depth, temperature, salinity, oxygen and shear stress were extracted from a coupled hydrodynamic and biogeochemical model of the Limfjorden, calibrated and validated over 2000--2016 \citep{DHI2019LimfjordenHD}. With the exception of depth, each was averaged over 2010--2016 and treated as temporally static. This period precedes the survey data (2018--2025), so the averages represent long-term conditions at each site rather than conditions during the years sampled. We assume that these long-term conditions constrain the persistence of cockle beds more strongly than interannual variation does. Sediment type was taken from the EMODnet Geology 1:250{,}000 seabed substrate map (\url{http://www.emodnet-geology.eu/}), which in the Limfjord is compiled by GEUS (\url{https://eng.geus.dk/mineral-resources/danish-raw-materials/seabed-sediment-map}). We used the four Folk classes present in the sampled area --- mud, muddy sand, sand and mixed sediment (Fig.~\ref{fig:predictors}f). Fishing intensity was the swept-area ratio of the cockle fishery, the area swept by the dredges divided by cell area, summed onto a 250~m grid for each fishing season from 2016--17 to 2024--25. Each station was assigned the swept-area ratio of the fishing season leading into its survey year, for instance 2020--21 for a survey in 2021. 

All predictors were extracted at the position of each station from their native rasters: depth at 50~m resolution, bottom temperature, salinity and oxygen at 100~m, maximum bottom shear stress at 200~m, sediment type at 100~m (rasterized from the 1:250{,}000 map, so its effective resolution is coarser), and fishing intensity at 250~m.

\subsection{Larval transport}

Larval dispersal was simulated with a coupled biophysical model combining the 3D hydrodynamic model MIKE 3 FM, the same model from which the environmental predictors were extracted (Section~\ref{sec:env}), with an agent-based module of MIKE ECO Lab that couples Lagrangian (agent-based) and Eulerian (concentration-based) formulations \citep{DHI2025_ECOLab}.

The hydrodynamic domain covered the whole of the Limfjorden on an unstructured flexible mesh, with a horizontal resolution ranging from under 100~m in narrow straits and inlets to 500~m in open areas, and 20 vertical layers comprising five sigma layers in the upper 5~m and 15 z-layers each 1~m thick. The model was forced at western and eastern open boundaries, connecting the Limfjorden to the North Sea and the Kattegat respectively, using water level, salinity and temperature from DHI's operational North Sea model (UKNS2) \citep{DHI2019NorthSea}. It was calibrated and validated against time series of salinity and temperature measured at 33 monitoring stations in the Limfjorden \citep{erichsen2019limfjorden}. 

The agent-based model of cockle larval dispersal is described in detail by \citet{hansenAssessingDemographicConnectivity2024}. Briefly, agents were released at the seabed on a 1~km grid covering the model domain (1{,}539 release locations), with one agent per location every 6~h through the spawning period from 1 May to 30 June in each year from 2010 to 2016, giving 381{,}421 agents per year. Constant horizontal (1~m$^2$~s$^{-1}$) and vertical (0.01~m$^2$~s$^{-1}$) dispersion coefficients accounted for hydrodynamics unresolved by the computational mesh. Agents were tracked for a pelagic larval duration of 35 days, comparable to values used in previous studies \citep[e.g.][]{cosciaFinescaleSeascapeGenomics2020}. Simulated larval supply from four example release locations is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:study_area}b.

Connectivity matrices were derived from the start and end positions of all simulated agents across the seven years (2010--2016), after dividing the Limfjorden into 2~$\times$~2~km grid cells. Each element of the raw matrix is the number of agents released in one cell (rows) whose end position fell in another cell, or in the same cell (columns). We converted this to a probability matrix by dividing each row by the total number of agents released from that source cell, including agents lost across the model boundaries, so that each element gives the probability that an agent released in one cell ends its pelagic phase in another. Further detail is given in \citet{hansenAssessingDemographicConnectivity2024}.

\subsection{Connectivity metrics}

\textcolor{red}{@ Ane check if you like it}
    
We used presence-weighted in-strength as our primary connectivity metric (Fig.~\ref{fig:predictors}f). In-strength sums the weights of incoming edges to a cell \citep{squartiniReciprocityWeightedNetworks2013}, representing the potential larval supply received by that cell. Without weighting, all source cells contribute to this measure regardless of their likelihood of being occupied by cockles. We therefore weighted the outgoing edges of each source cell by its predicted probability of cockle presence, so that cells with a higher probability of occurrence contributed more strongly to potential larval supply. The resulting metric represents potential settlement supplied by likely occupied cells while accounting for spatial variation in predicted cockle occurrence.

The presence probability of each source cell was predicted from a Bernoulli GLMM fitted to the survey presence--absence data with the same structure as the occurrence part of the species distribution models (Section~\ref{sec:sdm}), but without covariates: an intercept, survey and year as factors, and spatial random field, with a logit link. The model was fitted on the same barrier mesh as the species distribution models. Predictions were made at the centre of every 2 x 2 km grid cell containing at least one survey station, for the grab survey in the most recent year (2025).
Cells without survey stations were assigned a weight of zero. We deliberately excluded environmental covariates from this model because they were already used to explain cockle distribution in the SDMs. Including them here would introduce the same environmental signal into the connectivity metric, potentially counting its contribution twice when connectivity and habitat suitability were considered jointly. Because the network weights are predicted occurrence probabilities and the occurrence component has presence as its response, a cell's own predicted occurrence must not contribute to its in-strength. We therefore excluded self-supply when computing connectivity metrics, so that each cell's value derives only from other cells. At the 2~$\times$~2~km resolution of the connectivity grid this removes genuine transport between locations up to 2~km apart, so in-strength describes supply from other cells rather than total supply.

For robustness, and to test whether our conclusions depended on how connectivity was summarised, we calculated four further graph metrics on the same presence-weighted network: in-degree, in-closeness, eigenvector centrality and transitivity (Supplementary Fig.~S3). Whereas in-strength measures the amount of settlement a cell receives, these metrics describe the position of a cell within the dispersal network. In-degree is the number of source cells that send larvae to a given cell, including the cell itself when larvae are retained locally \citep{freemanCentralitySocialNetworks1978}. In-closeness is the inverse of the average shortest-path distance from cells that can reach a given cell \citep{estradaUSINGNETWORKCENTRALITY2008}. Edge probabilities $p$ were converted to distances as $-\log(p)$, such that the shortest path represents the most probable route through the network. Higher in-closeness therefore indicates cells that can be reached more readily through the network. Eigenvector centrality was calculated from the weighted, directed network, such that cells receiving larvae from highly central cells have greater centrality \citep{bonacichPowerCentralityFamily1987}. Transitivity is a graph metric that measures the proportion of possible triangular connections among the neighbours of a given node that are realised. It therefore quantifies how tightly connected the neighbourhood of a node is. Transitivity ranges from 0 to 1, with higher values indicating a greater proportion of realised triangular connections \citep{hansenAssessingDemographicConnectivity2024}.

All metrics were calculated in R using the \texttt{igraph} package \citep{csardi2006} following the graph construction of \citet{hansenAssessingDemographicConnectivity2024}. For every survey station we extracted each metric from the 2 × 2 km cell containing it.



\subsection{Species distribution models}
\label{sec:sdm}
We modelled cockle occurrence and biomass (g\,m$^{-2}$) with a two-part (hurdle) spatial generalized linear mixed model (GLMM). Because cockles were absent from most sampling stations and biomass was strongly right-skewed when present, the model comprised a binomial model for occurrence and a model for biomass where present. Because the surveys differ in what they record (Section~\ref{sec:surveys}), the two parts were fitted to different data: occurrence at all $n = 8{,}378$ stations of the three surveys, and biomass where present at the 1{,}220 grab stations with cockles. For an observation $y_{\mathbf{s},t}$ at location $\mathbf{s}$ in year $t$,
\begin{align}
\Pr(y_{\mathbf{s},t} > 0) &= p_{\mathbf{s},t}, &
\operatorname{logit}(p_{\mathbf{s},t}) &= \eta_{1,\mathbf{s},t},\\
y_{\mathbf{s},t} \mid y_{\mathbf{s},t} > 0 &\sim \operatorname{GG}\!\left(\mu_{\mathbf{s},t},\ \sigma,\ Q\right), &
\log(\mu_{\mathbf{s},t}) &= \eta_{2,\mathbf{s},t},
\end{align}
where $p_{\mathbf{s},t}$ is the probability of cockle occurrence and $\mu_{\mathbf{s},t}$ the expected biomass conditional on occurrence. Differences among gears in the area sampled and in catch efficiency were absorbed by survey effects on occurrence. We did not use the sample area as an offset. Under a complementary log-log link, an offset of log(sample area) assumes that the probability of detecting cockles grows in proportion to the area sampled, as it would if cockles were randomly scattered. Cockles are strongly aggregated and this assumption did not hold: within the suction-dredge survey, longer tows were not more likely to contain cockles, and models with area offsets fitted worse than a logit model without them ($\Delta$AIC $= +15.8$). Biomass where present followed a generalized gamma distribution (GG; \citet{prenticeLogGammaModel1974, dunicGeneralizedGammaFlexible2025}), which includes the gamma and lognormal distributions as special cases, with scale $\sigma$ and shape $Q$; it fitted the grab biomass better than a gamma distribution ($\Delta$AIC $=-15$) with residuals closer to uniform. The two parts had their own linear predictors,
\begin{align}
\eta_{1,\mathbf{s},t} &=
\underbrace{\mathbf{X}_{1,\mathbf{s},t}\,\boldsymbol{\beta}_1}_{\text{survey, year}}
+ \underbrace{E_{1,\mathbf{s}}}_{\text{environment}}
+ \underbrace{C_{1,\mathbf{s}}}_{\text{connectivity}}
+ \underbrace{\omega_{1,\mathbf{s}}}_{\text{spatial random field}},\\
\eta_{2,\mathbf{s},t} &=
\underbrace{\mathbf{X}_{2,\mathbf{s},t}\,\boldsymbol{\beta}_2}_{\text{year}}
+ \underbrace{\gamma\, f_{\mathbf{s},t}}_{\text{fishing}}
+ E_{2,\mathbf{s}} + C_{2,\mathbf{s}} + \omega_{2,\mathbf{s}},
\end{align}
where
\begin{align}
E_{m,\mathbf{s}} &= \sum_{k} \beta^{E}_{m,k}\, x_{k,\mathbf{s}},
&& \mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{s}} = (\text{depth},\ \text{depth}^2,\ \text{temperature},\ \text{oxygen},\ \text{salinity},\ \text{shear stress}\,[,\ \text{sediment}])_{\mathbf{s}},\\
C_{m,\mathbf{s}} &= \beta^{C}_m\, c_{\mathbf{s}},
&& c_{\mathbf{s}} = \text{connectivity metric},\\
\boldsymbol{\omega}_m &\sim \operatorname{MVN}\!\left(\mathbf{0}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\omega,m}\right).
\end{align}
Here $m \in \{1, 2\}$ denotes the occurrence and biomass parts, respectively. $\mathbf{X}_{1,\mathbf{s},t}$ contains an intercept and survey (grab as reference) and year as categorical effects, and $\mathbf{X}_{2,\mathbf{s},t}$ an intercept and year: survey was included only in the occurrence part, where it accounts for differences in sample area and catch efficiency among gears, since biomass was modelled for the grab survey alone. Year accounted for interannual differences in occurrence and biomass. $E_{m,\mathbf{s}}$ is the effect of the five continuous environmental predictors (Fig.~\ref{fig:predictors}a--e), with depth entering as a quadratic; sediment type (Fig.~\ref{fig:predictors}f; mud as reference) entered the occurrence part only, as only one grab station with cockles lay on sand. $f_{\mathbf{s},t}$ is fishing effort in the season preceding the survey, as $\log(1 + \text{swept-area ratio})$, included in the biomass part only as a control for depletion of fished beds; in the occurrence part it would mainly reflect fishers targeting existing beds. $C_{m,\mathbf{s}}$ is the effect of the connectivity value $c_{\mathbf{s}}$ of the 2~$\times$~2~km grid cell containing the sampling station (Fig.~\ref{fig:predictors}h). $\omega_{m,\mathbf{s}}$ is a spatially structured Gaussian random field with Matérn covariance, with spatial dependence characterized by a range parameter and marginal variance $\sigma^2_{\omega,m}$. The two parts share no parameters, so they were fitted separately; this is equivalent to a delta model in which the suction-dredge and KSKV stations contribute to the occurrence part only. Continuous predictors were standardized to zero mean and unit variance across all stations before model fitting. We calculated pairwise Spearman correlations among all continuous predictors, including connectivity metrics (Supplementary Fig.~S2). Correlations among the continuous predictors within a given model were all below $|\rho| = 0.7$.

Predictions of expected cockle biomass were obtained for one grab sample by multiplying the predicted probability of occurrence by the predicted biomass where present, for beds not fished in the preceding season. Prediction uncertainty was propagated by drawing 500 times from the joint parameter covariance matrix of each part and calculating expected biomass for each draw (Supplementary Fig.~S7).

\subsubsection{Spatial mesh}

Survey effort was uneven, with a few cockle beds sampled densely by grab in most years, while the rest of the fjord was covered more sparsely by dredge surveys (2018 suction dredge and KSKV). We therefore built a non-uniform mesh, with smaller triangles where sampling was dense and larger triangles where it was sparse. This keeps the SPDE approximation accurate where the data can resolve fine-scale variation, without wasting vertices elsewhere \citep{lindgren2018}. Given the complex, branching coastline of the Limfjord we used a barrier model \citep{bakkaNonstationaryGaussianModels2019}, setting the correlation range in mesh triangles over land to 10\% of the range in water. This forces spatial correlation to follow the water and decay rapidly across land (Supplementary Fig.~S4).


\subsubsection{Model comparison}

To quantify the contribution of environmental conditions and larval connectivity beyond residual spatial structure, we compared four model structures (Table~\ref{tab:model_structures}), all including the spatial random field ($\omega$), with environment ($E$) and connectivity ($C$) included in different combinations.

The same environmental and connectivity predictors were included in both parts, except sediment type (occurrence only). We first compared competing models using Akaike’s Information Criterion (AIC; \citet{akaike1974}), which evaluates relative in-sample model support while accounting for model complexity, with lower AIC values indicating greater support. We compared models for occurrence and for biomass where present, and for both together (the total AIC, the sum of the two, since the parts share no parameters).
To assess whether environmental conditions and connectivity improved predictions at locations not used for model fitting, we additionally compared the four model structures using spatially blocked 10-fold cross-validation \citep{roberts2017crossvalidation}. The spatial blocks were the 2~$\times$2 km grid cells of the connectivity model, such that all stations within a cell were held out together, and cells were randomly assigned to ten folds, which were kept identical across models (Supplementary Fig.~S5). Each model was fitted ten times, with one fold omitted in each iteration, and predictions were generated for the held-out observations. Prediction uncertainty was incorporated by sampling 500 times from the multivariate normal distribution defined by the maximum-likelihood estimates and their estimated covariance matrix. Models were compared using the expected log predictive density (ELPD) of the held-out observations \citep{vehtari2017practical}. The two parts were cross-validated separately. Because the biomass part was fitted to a subset of cells, it had its own folds, formed in the same way from the cells containing grab stations with cockles, so that each
fold held out a similar number of occupied cells. The total ELPD of each station was the sum of its occurrence term and, for grab stations with cockles, its biomass term. For each predictor block (environment or connectivity), we calculated the difference in ELPD between models with and without that block, together with its standard error across observations. Positive ELPD differences indicate improved out-of-sample predictive performance.

As a sensitivity analysis, we refitted the full model (Space + Environment + Connectivity) separately with each of four alternative connectivity metrics, replacing in-strength with the corresponding metric while keeping the remaining model specification unchanged, and compared the resulting models using AIC.

\subsubsection{Software}
We fit the spatial models with the R package \texttt{sdmTMB} \citep{andersonSdmTMBPackageFast2025}, development version 1.1.0.9024. sdmTMB uses automatic differentiation and the Laplace approximation from the R package TMB \citep{kristensenTMBAutomaticDifferentiation2016} and sparse matrix structures to set up the SPDE-based GMRFs from the R package \texttt{fmesher} \citep{lindgrenFmesherTriangleMeshes2025}. Estimation is done via maximum marginal likelihood. We confirmed that the nonlinear optimization was consistent with convergence by checking that the Hessian matrix was positive definite and the maximum absolute log-likelihood gradient with respect to fixed effects was <0.001 \citep{andersonSdmTMBPackageFast2025}. We assessed model fit with simulation-based residuals for each part. We simulated 500 biomass values at each station from the fitted part, drawing parameters from the multivariate normal distribution of the maximum-likelihood estimates, and computed each observation's quantile among its simulations with the R package \texttt{DHARMa} \citep{hartigDHARMaResidualDiagnostics2022}. We checked these residuals for uniformity with Q–Q plots and mapped them in space (Supplementary Fig.~S6). ELPD was calculated with the R package \texttt{loo} \citep{vehtari2017practical}. All analyses were done in R version 4.6.1 \citep{rcore2024}.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Cockles occur in a few dense beds, although larvae reach the whole fjord}

Cockles were present at 1{,}537 of 8{,}378 stations (18.3\%; 23\% of grab and suction-dredge stations, 9\% of KSKV stations), and biomass was concentrated in a few dense beds along the central channel and in the western and southern basins (Supplementary Fig.~S7). This patchiness was not matched by larval supply: simulated larvae dispersed widely from every release cell (Fig.~\ref{fig:study_area}b), and all but four stations received some larval input (presence-weighted in-strength $>0$). Larvae therefore reached areas both with and without established beds. Relative to a grab sample, the odds of recording cockles were 10.6 times higher in a suction-dredge tow (95\% CI 5.3--21.3) and 2.3 times higher in a KSKV tow (1.6--3.2), although these tows covered about 1{,}000 and 4{,}000 times the area of a grab.

\subsection{Habitat determines where cockles occur}

Occurrence was strongly structured by the environment (Table~\ref{tab:model-selection}). Adding the environmental predictors to the spatial field lowered the AIC of occurrence by 70.8 and improved prediction of occurrence in held-out grid cells ($\Delta$ELPD $=90.6$, SE $13.1$). The probability of occurrence was highest at intermediate depths, peaking at 7.0~m (95\% CI 5.7--7.9~m), increased with salinity ($0.76$ per SD on the logit scale; 95\% CI $0.37$ to $1.16$) and declined with temperature ($-0.48$; $-0.86$ to $-0.10$) and maximum shear stress ($-0.54$; $-0.87$ to $-0.21$) (Fig.~\ref{fig:coefficients}). It was lower on muddy sand than on mud ($-0.98$; $-1.44$ to $-0.53$), whereas sand and mixed sediment did not differ clearly from mud. Oxygen had no clear effect on occurrence ($-0.34$; $-0.75$ to $0.07$). Residual spatial autocorrelation decayed over a Mat\'ern range of 2.3~km (95\% CI 1.8--2.9~km), so the structure remaining after accounting for the environment was fine-scale relative to the extent of the fjord.

\subsection{Larval supply improves predictions of occurrence modestly, in the opposite direction to a larval subsidy}

Adding in-strength to the model containing environment and space lowered the AIC of occurrence by only 2.3, but improved prediction in held-out cells ($\Delta$ELPD $=18.9$, SE $5.2$; Table~\ref{tab:model-selection}). Without the environmental predictors, in-strength did not improve prediction ($\Delta$ELPD $=5.9$, SE $3.4$), so it carries predictive information only in combination with habitat covariates.

The direction of the effect was opposite to a larval-subsidy expectation: the probability of occurrence decreased with in-strength ($-0.28$, 95\% CI $-0.55$ to $-0.01$; Fig.~\ref{fig:coefficients}). In-strength was highest in deeper ($\rho=0.51$), less saline ($\rho=-0.53$) and more frequently hypoxic ($\rho=0.37$) areas, although also where shear stress was low ($\rho=-0.51$; Supplementary Fig.~S2), so larvae tended to accumulate in areas that our environmental model identifies as partly poor cockle habitat.

Results were similar across connectivity metrics. In-strength, chosen \emph{a priori}, also gave the lowest AIC for occurrence ($\Delta$AIC $+3.0$ to $+4.3$ for the four alternatives; Supplementary Table~S1), and no alternative metric had an occurrence coefficient whose confidence interval excluded zero (Supplementary Fig.~S8).

\subsection{Biomass among beds varies with year, recent fishing and oxygen}

Among grab stations with cockles, the full model (Space + Environment + Connectivity) had the lowest AIC for biomass ($\Delta$AIC $+3.0$ to $+10.7$ for the other structures; Table~\ref{tab:model-selection}). Biomass varied between years, from about half (2023) to about twice (2024) that in 2021, and was lower in beds fished in the preceding season ($-0.20$ per SD of $\log(1 + \text{swept-area ratio})$; 95\% CI $-0.24$ to $-0.15$), which applied to 44\% of the grab stations with cockles. Biomass also declined with oxygen ($-0.44$; $-0.83$ to $-0.04$) and temperature ($-0.66$; $-1.31$ to $-0.01$) and increased with salinity ($1.08$; $0.32$ to $1.84$), while its relationships with shear stress ($-0.23$; $-0.48$ to $0.01$) and depth were not clearly different from zero. Biomass was weakly lower where in-strength was high ($-0.19$; $-0.36$ to $-0.02$). The remaining spatial structure was weak, with a Mat\'ern range of 1.5~km (95\% CI 0.8--3.0~km) and a standard deviation of 0.40 on the log scale.

In cross-validation, however, neither block clearly improved prediction of biomass in held-out cells: adding the environment did not improve it ($\Delta$ELPD $=-5.9$, SE $6.9$, relative to Space), and adding in-strength improved it only within its standard error ($10.8$, SE $8.1$, relative to Space; $8.2$, SE $6.4$, relative to Space + Environment). Among the alternative connectivity metrics, eigenvector centrality was also negatively related to biomass ($-0.30$; $-0.55$ to $-0.04$), whereas transitivity was positively related ($0.43$; $0.05$ to $0.81$), and all were within 3.2 AIC units of in-strength (Supplementary Table~S1; Supplementary Fig.~S8).

Combining the two parts, the full model had the lowest total AIC ($\Delta$AIC $=5.3$ for Space + Environment) and the best total predictive performance (in-strength added to Space + Environment: $\Delta$ELPD $=27.1$, SE $8.6$; Table~\ref{tab:model-selection}).

\section{Discussion}
Simulated larvae reached nearly every one of the 8{,}378 surveyed stations in the Limfjord, yet cockles occurred at fewer than one in five stations. Occupancy was strongly related to the physical environment, whereas adding modelled connectivity to a model already containing environmental predictors and spatial structure improved prediction only modestly, and in the opposite direction to a larval subsidy: cockles were less likely to occur where predicted supply was highest. Among occupied beds, biomass varied between years and with recent fishing and oxygen, and the remaining fine-scale spatial structure was weak. Together, these results suggest that, at the spatial and temporal scales resolved here, larval supply does not constrain the distribution of cockles across the Limfjord. Instead, the principal spatial filter appears to occur after larvae have the opportunity to arrive, through the environmental conditions experienced at potential habitat.


This interpretation requires an important distinction between larval supply, settlement and recruitment. We simulated the first of these. The arrival of simulated larvae in a grid cell indicates that larvae could reach that location, but does not establish that they settled or survived after settlement. Larval behaviour and small-scale hydrodynamic processes can decouple dispersal from settlement \citep{pinedaCausesDecouplingLarval2010}, while post-settlement mortality can exceed 90\% in benthic invertebrates and operate over small spatial scales \citep{gosselinJuvenileMortalityBenthic1997, huntRoleEarlyPostsettlement1997}. A useful example comes from \textit{Ostrea lurida}, for which inner-estuarine sites received abundant larvae but produced few recruits because low salinity caused high post-settlement mortality, whereas outer sites were more supply-limited \citep{pritchardVariationLarvalSupply2016}. The surveys widen this gap further, because they record cockles only from about 9--12 months of age \citep{freitasSustainableCockleFishery2023}. Observed occurrence therefore reflects settlement together with a full year of post-settlement mortality, and spat that settled but died before the surveys go unrecorded. Our results cannot separate these processes, that is, whether larvae fail to settle in unsuitable parts of the Limfjord or settle and subsequently die. Either would produce what we observed: widespread larval supply but restricted occurrence.

The weak contribution of connectivity is therefore not evidence that dispersal is ecologically unimportant. Rather, it suggests that variation in larval supply does not explain much of the remaining spatial variation in cockle occupancy once habitat and spatial structure are accounted for. A similar pattern has been reported for other marine species. \citet{cecinoTestingInfluenceSeascape2021} incorporated network centrality metrics alongside environmental variables when modelling the distributions of a sea urchin and a snapper. Connectivity did not reduce predictive performance, but its contribution was substantially smaller than that of environmental predictors. Such results are consistent with the idea that connectivity may be permissive rather than limiting: larvae may need to reach suitable habitat, but once dispersal is sufficiently widespread, further variation in supply contributes little to explaining where adults occur.

Larval supply was negatively associated with both occurrence and biomass after accounting for habitat. The negative association between in-strength and habitat suitability provides additional support for this interpretation. In-strength was highest in deeper, less saline and more frequently hypoxic areas ($\rho = 0.51$, $-0.53$ and $0.37$, respectively), which were also among the least suitable areas according to our habitat model, although it was also highest where shear stress was low. Thus, areas receiving greater potential larval supply did not necessarily provide more suitable conditions for cockle persistence, indicating a spatial decoupling between connectivity and habitat suitability. Such decoupling is expected when the physical processes that transport and concentrate larvae differ from those determining post-settlement survival and population persistence \citep[e.g.][]{whiteScaleDependentChangesImportance2008}. This pattern was anticipated in the Limfjorden by \citet{hansenAssessingDemographicConnectivity2024}, who found high larval supply in areas where cockles were absent and suggested that environmental conditions may impose stronger constraints on population establishment than larval availability. Consequently, high larval supply may occur in locations where subsequent environmental filtering limits population establishment, survival, or persistence.

Biomass among occupied beds pointed to additional filters operating after occupancy has been established. Once biomass was modelled from the grab survey alone, the residual spatial structure was weak (range 1.5 km, standard deviation 0.40 on the log scale), and biomass varied instead between years and with fishing in the preceding season. This range is of the same order as the patchiness of cockles reported on intertidal flats  \citep{kraanPatchinessMacrobenthicInvertebrates2009}, suggesting that similar processes might structure beds in the subtidal Limfjorden. Biomass was about 20\% lower per standard deviation of recent fishing intensity, consistent with removal of biomass from fished beds, although fishers select beds by their density and condition, so part of this association may reflect where and when fishing took place rather than its effect. Dredging can also modify sediment and potentially reduce settlement, although the magnitude of such effects remains debated \citep{piersmaLongtermIndirectEffects2001, vandermeerReevaluationEffectsMechanical2023}. In the Limfjorden, however, fishing does not appear to prevent recruitment: settlement and adult beds recur in regularly fished areas, sometimes at the same locations (e.g. Salling Sund, K\aa{}s Bredning), and the most intensively fished basin held the highest cockle abundance in 2013–2023 \citep{freitasSustainableCockleFishery2023}. More fundamentally, standing biomass integrates demographic history. A bed’s standing biomass may reflect the recruitment and survival of successive cohorts, not simply the environmental conditions prevailing at the time of sampling. Recruitment can determine population abundance for years afterwards, depending on how successfully cohorts survive and grow. For example, \citet{magalhaesCocklePopulationDynamics2016} found that recruitment timing determined cohort density, lifespan and production in an unexploited cockle population, whereas adult spawner biomass did not predict subsequent recruitment. Density dependence may further shape these patterns. Adult cockles can reduce settlement and post-settlement survival through larviphagy and competition for food \citep{malhamReviewBiologyEuropean2012}, while competition for space and food can constrain growth and condition and increase susceptibility to disease. In the Limfjorden, one-year-old cockles are smaller at higher densities \citep{Freitas2024}, and slow juvenile growth has been linked to lower cohort survival in intertidal cockles \citep{montaudouinJuvenileGrowthDeficit2021}. In the Danish Wadden Sea, a single cohort of approximately 60{,}000 spat~m$^{-2}$ suppressed subsequent recruitment  before the population eventually disappeared \citep{f46a34c986624a1baee20a526e50e3c9}. Where a few strong year classes dominate the standing stock, spatial variation in present-day habitat may therefore explain little of the variation in biomass.

The estimated habitat relationships are best read as describing where cockle beds occur along the environmental gradients of the Limfjorden rather than as direct physiological responses. Salinity varied only between about 26 and 31~psu at the survey stations, well above the salinities at which newly settled juveniles experience high mortality \citep[about 15~psu;][]{peteiroResponsesSalinityStress2018} and far from the gradient along which \textit{Cerastoderma} species separate in the Baltic \citep{brockGeographicalDistributionCerastoderma1980}. Salinity and temperature co-varied along the fjord, and the temperature coefficients changed markedly when salinity was removed from the model, while the remaining effects did not. We therefore interpret the two together as the gradient in water masses between the North Sea and Kattegat ends of the Limfjord, which also carries differences in water exchange, stratification and stagnation \citep{schourup-kristensenDriversHypoxiaVariability2023}. Depth and shear stress likewise describe the physical setting of the beds more than a single mechanism. Occurrence peaked at intermediate depths and declined with shear stress, in line with other estuaries where cockle occurrence showed an optimum along current velocity \citep{ysebaertMacrobenthicSpeciesResponse2002} and was largely described by current velocity and emersion time \citep{katerDistributionCocklesCerastoderma2006}. Substrate is also a recognised driver: in the Schelde estuary, cockle occurrence peaked in fine sand with a median grain size of 100--150~$\mu$m \citep{ysebaertMacrobenthicSpeciesResponse2002}. In our data, however, occurrence was lower on muddy sand than on mud. The sediment map is a 1:250{,}000 compilation of surveys with different classification schemes, and nearly all grab stations with cockles lay in areas mapped as mud, so this association reflects the mapped sediment at a coarse scale rather than the sediment of individual beds and should not be strictly read as a sediment preference. Oxygen is the one variable whose effect fits a plausible mechanism. Cockles can tolerate concentrations considerably below our 4~mg~l$^{-1}$ threshold for days to weeks \citep{rosenbergHypoxicToleranceMarine1991}, so persistent exposure could reduce growth and condition without causing immediate mortality. This would affect the biomass of established beds more strongly than the probability of occurrence, consistent with oxygen contributing to the biomass part but not clearly to the occurrence part of our model.

Some limitations constrain the strength and scale of these conclusions. First, sampling effort was highly uneven by design. A small number of beds were sampled intensively with grabs, whereas dredge surveys covered much of the remaining fjord more sparsely, and the gears differ in sampled area and efficiency. Because standardised sampling across the entire fjord was unavailable for this period, we used each survey for what it can record: all surveys for occurrence, with survey-specific effects, and the grab survey alone for biomass. The biomass relationships therefore apply to the beds sampled by the grab surveys, and the survey effects assume that the difference in detection between gears does not vary with conditions such as sediment, which we could not test. We also refined the mesh in densely sampled areas. Nevertheless, residual differences in detectability or sampling design could contribute to the spatial structure attributed to the model.

Second, the spatial resolution of the connectivity estimates limits the scale at which connectivity effects can be detected. Connectivity was calculated on 2~$\times$2~km cells, approximately matching the scale of the residual spatial fields. Connectivity effects operating at substantially finer scales would therefore be averaged out. In addition, the connectivity estimates inherit the spatial resolution and assumptions of the underlying hydrodynamic model, which can influence simulated dispersal as much as biological parameterisation \citep{sciasciaLarvalDispersalSimulations2022,saint-amandBiophysicalModelsResolution2023}. Our results should consequently be interpreted as evidence against a strong broad-scale connectivity signal at the resolution considered, rather than against connectivity effects in general.

Third, both connectivity and environmental predictors were summarised as 2010–2016 means, while survey years were represented by factor effects. The analysis therefore addresses the spatial association between average larval supply and average habitat conditions over the survey period, rather than whether year-specific larval supply predicts subsequent recruitment. A time-varying connectivity model could test whether annual mismatches between larval supply and environmental conditions generate recruitment responses, although the relatively short survey series currently limits the information available for such an analysis. Similarly, our connectivity metric describes potential larval supply rather than realised settlement, and thus cannot distinguish dispersal limitation from mortality or other post-settlement processes.

Spatial confounding is another consideration when interpreting the relative contribution of connectivity and the spatial field. A spatially structured covariate and a spatial random field can compete to explain the same variation, potentially biasing covariate estimates and reallocating variation between model components \citep{huiSpatialConfoundingJoint2024,makinenSpatialConfoundingBayesian2022}. The extent of this competition depends on the spatial scales of the processes involved \citep{paciorekImportanceScaleSpatialConfounding2010}. In our case, the fitted spatial fields had short ranges and therefore primarily absorbed fine-scale residual variation, whereas connectivity and the environmental predictors varied more smoothly across the fjord. This separation of scales should reduce, although not eliminate, competition between the connectivity covariate and the residual field. Importantly, the negative relationship between in-strength and habitat suitability is also inconsistent with a simple interpretation in which the spatial field merely absorbs a positive connectivity effect. Nevertheless, the possibility of scale-dependent confounding means that the absence of a strong connectivity signal should not be interpreted as definitive evidence that connectivity has no effect.

From a management perspective, these results suggest that improving habitat conditions may be more effective than targeting larval source areas when larval supply is already widespread. Simulated larvae reached essentially the entire survey domain, meaning that increasing spawning biomass in particular source areas is unlikely, by itself, to expand the area available to cockles if larvae already reach those locations. Protecting established beds from physical disturbance and chronic deoxygenation may therefore be more direct management levers. This conclusion is conditional on larval supply being sufficiently widespread. Where supply is limiting, connectivity can become much more important. For example, \citet{mcgeadyLongtermInterannualVariability2022} found that year-specific larval dispersal indices predicted adult density in the Norway lobster (\textit{Nephrops norvegicus}) grounds with consistently low larval supply, but not in grounds where supply was consistently high. Connectivity-informed management is therefore most compelling where dispersal actually constrains population establishment \citep{griecoDesigningDispersalConnectivityinformed2026}.

The importance of connectivity should therefore depend on whether larvae limit access to suitable habitat. Where dispersal is pervasive, larval supply becomes similar among sites and little spatial variation remains for connectivity to explain, whereas isolated or weakly connected systems can remain strongly structured by dispersal limitation
\citep{heinoMetacommunityOrganisationSpatial2015}. Connectivity can also matter for a different reason, by limiting whether populations reach suitable habitat they do not yet occupy. \citet{assisOceanographicConnectivityStrongly2026} projected future ranges of marine forest species from environmental suitability and then removed the suitable areas that modelled larvae could not reach, which substantially reduced projected expansion. Connectivity there constrained access
to new habitat rather than competing with habitat inside the occupied range.

The Limfjord lies nearer the other end of this gradient. Simulated larvae
reach essentially the whole surveyed area, and environmental conditions restrict where cockles occur within it. Assuming that larvae can access suitable habitat may therefore be reasonable here, but it remains an assumption, and one worth testing in systems where dispersal is more restricted \citep{barveCrucialRoleAccessible2011}. Widespread larval dispersal need not produce widespread adult populations. For cockles in the Limfjord, the conditions larvae encounter on arrival seem to matter more than spatial variation in predicted larval supply.
