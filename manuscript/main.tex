\section*{Abstract}

%Correlative species distribution models for sessile marine organisms generally assume unrestricted drift and access by eggs and larvae to all suitable habitats. While such propagule supply can now be estimated from biophysical transport models it is rarely tested explicitly in distribution models. Here we combined high-resolution surveys of cockles (\textit{Cerastoderma} spp.) in Limfjorden, Denmark (2018–2025), with a particle-tracking simulation of larval dispersal to investigate the effect and relative importance of connectivity in habitat-based spatial models of biomass. We compared six graph-theoretic connectivity metrics alongside environmental predictors and a spatial random field. Model selection provided only modest support for including connectivity ($\Delta$AIC = 11.6), but its contribution remained small: the spatial random field explained 60.3–64.9\% of the variance, environmental predictors 15.1–17.0\%, and connectivity only 0.9–2.9\%. Surprisingly, all six connectivity metrics entered the model with negative coefficients, opposite to the expectation that greater larval supply should increase adult biomass. These patterns were consistent for both biomass and occurrence models. Our results indicate that environmental conditions and residual spatial structure dominate the distribution of cockle biomass in Limfjorden, whereas modelled larval connectivity provides only modest additional explanatory power. More broadly, our study provides a framework for testing, rather than assuming, the role of dispersal in correlative species distribution models.

\section{Introduction}

The common cockle \textit{Cerastoderma spp.} supports one of the most valuable bivalve fishery in Denmark ... \textcolor{red}{Pedro, Camille, maybe we can use some information form you report}
... Managing this fishery, and identifying where depleted beds might recover, requires understanding what determines where cockles occur and how much biomass they support.

For sessile marine invertebrates with pelagic larvae, adult distributions are set by two distinct processes: whether a location provides suitable habitat, and whether larvae reach it. Correlative species distribution models (SDMs) generally address only the first. By relating occurrence or biomass to environmental covariates, they implicitly assume that every environmentally suitable location is reachable, and therefore that propagule supply does not limit distribution \citep{barveCrucialRoleAccessible2011}. For species whose entire dispersal capacity is confined to a pelagic larval stage lasting days to weeks, this is a substantive ecological assumption rather than a technical one, yet it is rarely tested explicitly.

The distinction carries direct management consequences. If larval supply limits occupancy, habitat-only models will predict suitable but unoccupied locations, overestimating the area available for recovery and potentially directing restoration toward sites that larvae seldom reach \citep{griecoDesigningDispersalConnectivityinformed2026}. If instead larval supply is effectively ubiquitous within a system, habitat-only models are adequate, connectivity adds little predictive value, and management effort might be better spent on habitat condition and on protecting spawning biomass.

Larval supply is not uniform across a system. Transport is shaped by ocean circulation, larval traits and behaviour, and the spatial arrangement of suitable habitat, and can therefore generate substantial heterogeneity among locations in the number of larvae that arrive \citep{cowenLarvalDispersalMarine2009, siegelStochasticNatureLarval2008}. Biophysical dispersal models represent this exchange as a connectivity network, in which habitat patches are nodes and simulated larval exchange forms links. Graph theory then provides node-level metrics summarising how much larval supply a patch receives and where it sits within that network, giving spatially explicit estimates of potential larval supply that can complement environmental predictors and be incorporated directly into SDMs. Connectivity metrics have been used to identify source and sink areas and to inform conservation and restoration planning \citep{hansenAssessingDemographicConnectivity2024, griecoDesigningDispersalConnectivityinformed2026}, but how much they add beyond environmental predictors and spatial structure has rarely been assessed \citep[but see][]{cecinoTestingInfluenceSeascape2021}.

In the Limfjorden specifically, \citet{hansenAssessingDemographicConnectivity2024} used a biophysical model and graph-theoretic metrics to characterise cockle connectivity, showing that commercially productive beds depend largely on larval import from unexploited spawning biomass elsewhere rather than on self-recruitment, while other areas are comparatively isolated and rely on local retention. They further observed that some areas predicted to receive substantial settlement support no cockle populations, and suggested that environmental factors may be more limiting there than larval availability. Whether that is so has not been tested directly.

Testing the contribution of connectivity is challenging because dispersal and habitat are not independent processes. Connectivity is shaped by the same hydrodynamic processes that influence environmental conditions and can therefore be correlated with habitat predictors. In addition, connectivity metrics are often spatially structured and may capture broad-scale spatial patterns not represented by measured environmental variables --- patterns that a spatial random field would otherwise absorb. Apparent effects of connectivity may therefore reflect environmental covariation or residual spatial structure rather than a direct contribution of larval supply. Explicitly accounting for both environmental predictors and spatial structure is consequently important when assessing whether connectivity provides additional explanatory power.

Here we combine extensive survey data for \textit{Cerastoderma} spp. in the Limfjorden (7{,}468 stations, 2018–2025) with a biophysical connectivity model to ask: (i) is cockle biomass higher at sites receiving greater larval supply, once habitat is accounted for? (ii) can any such effect be distinguished from environmental gradients and residual spatial structure? and (iii) is the answer robust to how connectivity is summarised?

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Study system}

The Limfjorden is a relatively large, shallow, semi-enclosed estuarine system crossing the Jutland Peninsula in northern Denmark, connecting the North Sea to the west with the Kattegat to the east. It comprises a series of shallow basins linked by narrow straits and channels, a configuration that produces substantial spatial variation in water exchange and circulation \citep{hofmeisterThreedimensionalModelStudy2009}. Mean depth is approximately 4.5~m and the tidal range is small, around 0.2~m \citep{wilesStratificationMixingLimfjorden2006, dolmerEvaluationDanishMussel2002}. The system is eutrophic, with high nutrient loading from an agricultural catchment, and supports extensive benthic bivalve populations, including the cockle stocks considered here and a long-established blue mussel (\textit{Mytilus edulis}) fishery and aquaculture industry \citep{freitasSustainableCockleFishery2023, maarIntensifiedMusselFarming2023, dolmerEvaluationDanishMussel2002}. Summer deoxygenation events occur regularly and vary in extent across the system \citep{schourup-kristensenDriversHypoxiaVariability2023}.

Previous biophysical modelling indicates substantial larval transport among areas of the Limfjorden despite this hydrographic heterogeneity. For blue mussels, simulations show larvae dispersing across hydrographically distinct parts of the system \citep{pastorAgentbasedModelingGenetics2021, pastorSensitivityAnalysisMussel2023}, and genetic evidence supports this, indicating that the fjord functions as a relatively well-connected system for that species \citep{pastorAgentbasedModelingGenetics2021}. For cockles, \citet{hansenAssessingDemographicConnectivity2024} found spatial variation in demographic connectivity within the fjord, with some beds depending on larval import from elsewhere and others on local retention.

\subsection{Connectivity metrics}

\textcolor{red}{@ Ane check if you like it}

The biophysical model simulated larval exchange among 2 $\times$ 2 km grid cells across the Limfjorden. Its output was a matrix of larval counts settling between each pair of cells. We divided each count by the number of larvae released from its source cell, so that every edge gives the probability that a larva released in one cell settles in another.
    
We used presence-weighted in-strength as our primary connectivity metric. In-strength sums the weights of incoming edges to a cell \citep{squartiniReciprocityWeightedNetworks2013}, representing the potential larval supply received by that cell. Without weighting, all source cells contribute to this measure regardless of their likelihood of being occupied by cockles. We therefore weighted the outgoing edges of each source cell by its predicted probability of cockle presence, so that cells with a higher probability of occurrence contributed more strongly to potential larval supply. The resulting metric represents potential settlement supplied by likely occupied cells while accounting for spatial variation in predicted cockle occurrence.

The presence probability of each source cell was predicted from a Bernoulli GLMM fitted to the survey presence--absence data, containing only an intercept, survey and year as factors, and a spatial random field. The model was fitted using the same approach and barrier mesh as the species distribution models (Section~\ref{sec:sdm}). Predictions were made at the centre of every 2$\times$2~km grid cell containing at least one survey station, for the grab survey in the most recent year (2025). Cells without survey stations were assigned a weight of zero. We deliberately excluded environmental covariates from this model because they were already used to explain cockle distribution in the SDMs. Including them here would introduce the same environmental signal into the connectivity metric, potentially counting its contribution twice when connectivity and habitat suitability were considered jointly. We weighted by presence probability rather than biomass to avoid using information derived from biomass predictions as a predictor of biomass.

For robustness, and to test whether our conclusions depended on how connectivity was summarised, we calculated four further graph metrics on the same presence-weighted network: in-degree, in-closeness, eigenvector centrality and transitivity. Whereas in-strength measures the amount of settlement a cell receives, these metrics describe the position of a cell within the dispersal network. In-degree is the number of source cells that send larvae to a given cell, including the cell itself when larvae are retained locally \citep{freemanCentralitySocialNetworks1978}. In-closeness is the inverse of the average shortest-path distance from cells that can reach a given cell \citep{estradaUSINGNETWORKCENTRALITY2008}. Edge probabilities $p$ were converted to distances as $-\log(p)$, such that the shortest path represents the most probable route through the network. Higher in-closeness therefore indicates cells that can be reached more readily through the network. Eigenvector centrality was calculated from the weighted, directed network, such that cells receiving larvae from highly central cells have greater centrality \citep{bonacichPowerCentralityFamily1987}. Transitivity is a graph metric that measures the proportion of possible triangular connections among the neighbours of a given node that are realised. It therefore quantifies how tightly connected the neighbourhood of a node is. Transitivity ranges from 0 to 1, with higher values indicating a greater proportion of realised triangular connections \citep{hansenAssessingDemographicConnectivity2024}.

All metrics were calculated in R using the \texttt{igraph} package \citep{csardi2006} following the graph construction of \citet{hansenAssessingDemographicConnectivity2024}. For every survey station we extracted each metric from the 2 × 2 km cell containing it.



\subsection{Species distribution models}
\label{sec:sdm}
We modelled cockle biomass density (g\,m$^{-2}$) using a spatial delta-gamma (hurdle) generalized linear mixed model (GLMM). Because cockles were absent from most sampling stations and biomass was strongly right-skewed when present, the model comprised two components: a binomial model for cockle occurrence and a Gamma model for positive biomass. For an observation $y_{\mathbf{s},t}$ at location $\mathbf{s}$ in year $t$ ($n = 8{,}137$),
\begin{align}
\Pr(y_{\mathbf{s},t} > 0) &= p_{\mathbf{s},t}, &
\operatorname{logit}(p_{\mathbf{s},t}) &= \eta_{1,\mathbf{s},t},\\
y_{\mathbf{s},t} \mid y_{\mathbf{s},t} > 0 &\sim \operatorname{Gamma}\!\left(\phi,\ \mu_{\mathbf{s},t}/\phi\right), &
\log(\mu_{\mathbf{s},t}) &= \eta_{2,\mathbf{s},t},
\end{align}
where $p_{\mathbf{s},t}$ is the probability of cockle occurrence, $\mu_{\mathbf{s},t}$ is the expected biomass conditional on occurrence, $\phi$ is the Gamma shape parameter, and  $\mu_{\mathbf{s},t}^2/\phi$ is the variance. Each component had its own linear predictor,
\begin{equation}
\eta_{m,\mathbf{s},t} =
\underbrace{\mathbf{X}_{\mathbf{s},t}\,\boldsymbol{\beta}_m}_{\text{survey, year}}
+ \underbrace{E_{m,\mathbf{s}}}_{\text{environment}}
+ \underbrace{C_{m,\mathbf{s}}}_{\text{connectivity}}
+ \underbrace{\omega_{m,\mathbf{s}}}_{\text{spatial random field}},
\end{equation}
where
\begin{align}
E_{m,\mathbf{s}} &= \sum_{k=1}^{6} \beta^{E}_{m,k}\, x_{k,\mathbf{s}},
&& \mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{s}} = (\text{depth},\ \text{depth}^2,\ \text{temperature},\ \text{oxygen},\ \text{salinity},\ \text{shear stress})_{\mathbf{s}},\\
C_{m,\mathbf{s}} &= \beta^{C}_m\, c_{\mathbf{s}},
&& c_{\mathbf{s}} = \text{connectivity metric},\\
\boldsymbol{\omega}_m &\sim \operatorname{MVN}\!\left(\mathbf{0}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\omega,m}\right).
\end{align}
Here $m \in \{1, 2\}$ denotes the occurrence and positive-biomass components, respectively. The model matrix $\mathbf{X}_{\mathbf{s},t}$ contains an intercept and survey and year as categorical effects, with coefficients $\boldsymbol{\beta}_m$. Survey was included to account for differences in catch efficiency among the three gears, while year accounted for interannual differences in cockle occurrence and biomass. $E_{m,\mathbf{s}}$ is the effect of the five environmental predictors (Fig.~\ref{fig:predictors}a--e), with depth entering as a quadratic, and $C_{m,\mathbf{s}}$ is the effect of the connectivity value $c_{\mathbf{s}}$ of the 2~$\times$~2~km grid cell containing the sampling station (Fig.~\ref{fig:predictors}f). $\omega_{m,\mathbf{s}}$ is a spatially structured Gaussian random field with Matérn covariance, with spatial dependence characterized by a range parameter and marginal variance $\sigma^2_{\omega,m}$. The two components had separate fixed effects and spatial random fields, allowing the environmental and connectivity effects on cockle occurrence to differ from those on biomass conditional on occurrence. Continuous predictors were standardized to zero mean and unit variance before model fitting.

Predictions of expected cockle biomass were obtained by multiplying the predicted probability of occurrence by the predicted positive biomass. Prediction uncertainty was propagated by drawing from the joint parameter covariance matrix and calculating expected biomass for each draw.

\subsubsection{Spatial mesh}

Survey effort was uneven, with a few cockle beds sampled densely by grab in most years, while the rest of the fjord was covered more sparsely by dredge surveys (2018 suction dredge and KSKV). We therefore built a non-uniform mesh, with smaller triangles where sampling was dense and larger triangles where it was sparse. This keeps the SPDE approximation accurate where the data can resolve fine-scale variation, without wasting vertices elsewhere \citep{lindgren2018}. Because Limfjorden is a narrow, branching fjord we used a barrier model \citep{bakkaNonstationaryGaussianModels2019}, setting the correlation range in mesh triangles over land to 10\% of the range in water. This forces spatial correlation to follow the water and decay rapidly across land (Supplementary Fig~S2).


\subsubsection{Model comparison}

To quantify the contribution of environmental conditions and larval connectivity beyond residual spatial structure, we compared four model structures (Table~\ref{tab:model_structures}), all including the spatial random field ($\omega$), with environment ($E$) and connectivity ($C$) included in different combinations.

The same set of predictors was included in both model components (occurrence and positive biomass). We first compared competing models using Akaike’s Information Criterion (AIC; \citet{akaike1974}), which evaluates relative in-sample model support while accounting for model complexity, with lower AIC values indicating greater support.

To assess whether environmental conditions and connectivity improved predictions at locations not used for model fitting, we additionally compared the four model structures using spatially blocked 10-fold cross-validation \citep{roberts2017crossvalidation}. The spatial blocks were the 2~$\times$2km grid cells of the connectivity model, such that all stations within a cell were held out together, and cells were randomly assigned to ten folds, which were kept identical across models (Supplementary Fig. S3). Each model was fitted ten times, with one fold omitted in each iteration, and predictions were generated for the held-out observations. Prediction uncertainty was incorporated by sampling from the multivariate normal distribution defined by the maximum-likelihood estimates and their estimated covariance matrix. Models were compared using the expected log predictive density (ELPD) of the held-out observations \citep{vehtari2017practical}. For each predictor block (environment or connectivity), we calculated the difference in ELPD between models with and without that block, together with its standard error across observations. Positive ELPD differences indicate improved out-of-sample predictive performance.

As a sensitivity analysis, we refitted the full model (Space + Environment + Connectivity) separately with each of four alternative connectivity metrics, replacing in-strength with the corresponding metric while keeping the remaining model specification unchanged, and compared the resulting models using AIC.

\subsubsection{Software}
We fit the spatial models with the R package \texttt{sdmTMB} \citep{andersonSdmTMBPackageFast2025}, development version 1.1.0.9024. sdmTMB uses automatic differentiation and the Laplace approximation from the R package TMB \citep{kristensenTMBAutomaticDifferentiation2016} and sparse matrix structures to set up the SPDE-based GMRFs from the R package \texttt{fmesher} \citep{lindgrenFmesherTriangleMeshes2025}. Estimation is done via maximum marginal likelihood. We confirmed that the nonlinear optimization was consistent with convergence by checking that the Hessian matrix was positive definite and the maximum absolute log-likelihood gradient with respect to fixed effects was <0.001 \citep{andersonSdmTMBPackageFast2025}. We assessed model residuals using Q–Q plots and by plotting their spatial distribution (Supplementary Fig.~S4). ELPD was calculated with the R package \texttt{loo} \citep{vehtari2017practical}. All analyses were done in R version 4.6.1 \citep{rcore2024}.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Model selection}

Model comparison supported the environmental predictors but not connectivity (Table~\ref{tab:model-selection}). The Space + Environment model had the lowest AIC, and adding in-strength to it did not improve the fit ($\Delta$AIC $=0.1$). Removing the environmental predictors from the full model increased AIC by $\approx$62 (Space + Connectivity), and the Space-only model was $\approx$59 AIC units worse than Space + Environment.

Cross-validation gave the same ranking out of sample (Table~\ref{tab:model-selection}). Adding the environment improved prediction of held-out grid cells, both when added to the spatial field ($\Delta$ELPD $=86.2$, SE $16.4$) and when added to the spatial field and in-strength ($101.1$, SE $17.7$). Adding in-strength showed little evidence of improving predictive performance and, if anything, reduced it, both when added to the spatial field ($-36.0$, SE $10.9$) and when added to the spatial field and the environment ($-20.7$, SE $12.6$).

Replacing in-strength with any of the four alternative metrics did not change these conclusions. None of them improved the fit of the full model ($\Delta$AIC relative to in-strength: $+1.7$ for eigenvector centrality, $+3.4$ for transitivity and $+3.6$ for in-degree and in-closeness; Supplementary~Table~S1, and none had a coefficient whose CI excluded zero in either component (Supplementary Fig.~S5).

\subsection{Effects of habitat, connectivity and space}

The environment mainly determined where cockles occurred rather than how much biomass they reached where present (Fig.~\ref{fig:coefficients}). In the full model, the probability of presence peaked at intermediate depths (depth$^2$: $\hat{\beta}=-0.30$, 95\% CI $-0.43$ to $-0.17$ per SD), with a maximum at 7.1~m (95\% CI 5.9--8.0~m) and declining towards both shallower and deeper water (Fig.~\ref{fig:coefficients}b); biomass where present showed no depth optimum. The probability of presence also increased with salinity ($0.82$, $0.43$ to $1.21$), and declined with temperature ($-0.51$, $-0.89$ to $-0.13$) and maximum shear stress ($-0.68$, $-0.99$ to $-0.36$); the CIs for linear depth and oxygen included zero. Biomass where present declined with maximum shear stress ($-0.31$, $-0.61$ to $-0.01$) and with the number of days of low oxygen ($-0.59$, $-1.14$ to $-0.05$); the other environmental CIs included zero. Beyond these environmental gradients, residual spatial autocorrelation decayed over a Mat\'ern range of 2.2~km (95\% CI 1.8--2.8~km) for presence and 2.7~km (1.8--4.0~km) for biomass where present, i.e.\ over about one grid cell.

The survey effects were large, especially for biomass where present: relative to the 2021--2025 grab survey, biomass where present was lower by a factor of about 36 in the KSKV dredge survey ($-3.58$ on the log scale, $-3.88$ to $-3.28$) and about 78 in the 2018 suction-dredge survey ($-4.36$, $-5.01$ to $-3.71$), whereas cockles were more often recorded as present with the dredges (KSKV: $0.75$, $0.40$ to $1.10$; 2018: $2.45$, $1.74$ to $3.16$), consistent with the larger area sampled per station.

Contrary to the expectation that greater larval supply should increase adult abundance, the in-strength coefficient was negative in both components, and its CI included zero (presence: $-0.27$, $-0.55$ to $0.00$; biomass where present: $-0.04$, $-0.27$ to $0.20$; Fig.~\ref{fig:coefficients}a); across the observed range of in-strength, predicted presence and biomass where present changed little relative to their uncertainty (Fig.~\ref{fig:coefficients}b).
