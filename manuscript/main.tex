% ---- ABSTRACT --------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Abstract}
% ~150--300 words. State the question, the data/approach, the key findings,
% and why they matter.
Correlative species distribution models for sessile marine invertebrates generally assume unrestricted larval access to all suitable habitat, yet propagule supply is computable from biophysical transport models and is rarely tested as a predictor. We combined a 7{,}468-station survey of the common cockle \textit{Cerastoderma spp} in the Limfjorden, Denmark (2018--2025), with a particle-tracking simulation of larval dispersal to ask whether connectivity metrics improve a habitat-based spatial GLMM of cockle standing stock. A spatial random field accounted for 61\% of the total variance in biomass and the environmental predictors --- depth, salinity, temperature, oxygen and shear stress --- for 16\%, whereas five alternative connectivity metrics added only 1.0--2.9\%, the smallest explained component in every model. Connectivity did improve fit ($\Delta$AIC = 11.6), but entered with consistently negative coefficients (in-strength $\hat{\beta} = -0.41$, 95\% CI $-0.63$ to $-0.18$), opposite in sign to a larval-subsidy expectation, and had almost no marginal association with biomass ($|\rho| < 0.12$ for every metric). Modelled supply was strongly collinear with the fjord's habitat geography ($\rho = 0.59$ with depth, $-0.67$ with salinity), so the best-connected cells are the deep, brackish inner basins in which cockles were least abundant. The result was insensitive to which year of the dispersal simulation was used, but two purely topological metrics reversed sign when the spatial field was removed. \ml{[Optional, if the co-authors want the species-sorting argument: independent genetic and agent-based-modelling evidence that the Limfjorden is a single well-connected unit would support an interpretation in which post-settlement processes, not larval delivery, set adult distribution.]} Rather than a case against connectivity in distribution models, we present two computable diagnostics --- the collinearity of modelled supply with the environmental predictors, and the stability of connectivity coefficients to a spatial random field --- that indicate whether a connectivity covariate can inform a distribution model in a given system.

\section{Introduction}
@ all, feel free to add any point or develop what you are most comfortable with:

{\color{red}

\begin{itemize}

    \item Correlative SDMs assume all environmentally suitable locations are reachable, implying that propagule supply does not limit species distributions. For sessile marine invertebrates with pelagic larvae, however, adult distributions depend on both habitat suitability and larval supply, an assumption that has rarely been tested because connectivity has historically been unobservable.

    \item If dispersal limits occupancy, habitat-only SDMs will overpredict suitable but unoccupied locations, with implications for restoration, marine spatial planning, stock assessment, and forecasting climate-driven range shifts. Conversely, if larval supply is effectively ubiquitous, habitat-only models are sufficient and connectivity adds little predictive value.

    \item Advances in biophysical dispersal modelling and network theory now make this assumption directly testable by deriving connectivity matrices and graph-based metrics (e.g. in-strength, in-degree, centrality) that can be incorporated as SDM covariates. Despite this, most studies use connectivity descriptively rather than testing whether it explains species distributions beyond habitat.

    \item A key challenge is that connectivity is intrinsically linked to the same hydrodynamic processes that generate environmental gradients, making connectivity metrics potentially collinear with habitat predictors and spatially smooth enough to proxy residual spatial structure. Consequently, apparent connectivity effects may disappear once environmental covariates or spatial random effects are included.

    \item Using an extensive survey of the common cockle \textit{Cerastoderma spp} in Limfjorden (7,468 stations; 2018--2025) together with a biophysical connectivity model, we ask: (i) does connectivity improve habitat-based SDMs of cockle biomass? (ii) are inferred effects robust across alternative connectivity metrics? and (iii) can connectivity be distinguished from environmental gradients and residual spatial structure?

\end{itemize}

}

\subsection{Species distribution models}

We modelled cockle biomass density (g\,m$^{-2}$) as a function of environmental conditions, survey type, larval connectivity, and residual spatial autocorrelation using spatial generalized linear mixed models (GLMMs). Biomass density ($y_i$) at survey station $i$ ($n = 7{,}468$) was modelled using a Tweedie distribution (compound Poisson--gamma; $1<p<2$) with a log link,
\begin{equation}
y_i \sim \operatorname{Tweedie}(\mu_i,\phi,p), \qquad
\log(\mu_i)=\eta_i ,
\end{equation}
where the Tweedie distribution accommodates zero-valued and continuous positive biomass observations within a single modelling framework.

The linear predictor comprised additive effects of survey type, environmental conditions, larval connectivity, and a spatial random field,
\begin{equation}
\eta_i
=
\beta_0
+
\underbrace{G_i}_{\text{survey}}
+
\underbrace{E_i}_{\text{environment}}
+
\underbrace{C_i}_{\text{connectivity}}
+
\underbrace{\omega_{s_i}}_{\text{space}} ,
\end{equation}
where
\begin{align}
G_i &= \gamma_{g(i)},
&& g(i)\in\{\text{Commercial},\text{Stock}\},
\quad \gamma_{\text{Commercial}}=0,\\
E_i &= \sum_{k=1}^{5}\beta_k x_{ik},
&& \mathbf{x}_i=(\text{depth},\,\text{temperature},\,\text{oxygen},\,\text{salinity},\,\text{shear stress})_i,\\
C_i &= \theta c_i,
&& c_i=\text{connectivity metric},\\
\bm{\omega}
&\sim
\mathrm{MVN}\!\left(\mathbf{0},\bm{\Sigma}_{\omega}\right),
&&
\bm{\Sigma}_{\omega}\ \text{defined by a Mat\'ern covariance function with range }\rho\text{ and marginal SD }\sigma_{\omega}.
\end{align}

Here, $\beta_0$ is the intercept, $\gamma_{g(i)}$ accounts for differences between commercial and stock assessment surveys, $\mathbf{x}_i$ contains the five environmental predictors, $c_i$ is the connectivity value assigned to the grid cell containing station $i$, and $\omega_{s_i}$ is a Gaussian random field representing residual spatial autocorrelation not explained by the fixed effects. All continuous predictors, including environmental variables and connectivity metrics, were standardized to zero mean and unit variance before model fitting.

Because no single connectivity metric was assumed to best represent larval connectivity, we fitted separate models for each of the six candidate connectivity metrics, replacing the connectivity term ($C_i$) in the linear predictor while keeping all other model components unchanged.

To assess whether the effects differed between biomass and occurrence, we additionally fitted Bernoulli GLMMs to presence--absence data ($z_i\in\{0,1\}$) using a logit link,
\[
z_i \sim \operatorname{Bernoulli}(\pi_i), \qquad
\operatorname{logit}(\pi_i)=\eta_i,
\]
with the same linear predictor. Results from these models are presented in the Supporting Information.

\subsubsection*{Model structures comparison}

To quantify the relative contribution of environmental conditions, larval connectivity, and residual spatial structure, we compared six model structures (Table~\ref{tab:model_structures}). The survey type effect ($G$) was included in all models, whereas the environment ($E$), connectivity ($C$), and spatial random field ($\omega$) were included in different combinations.

Models containing the connectivity term ($C$; structures 4--6 in Table~\ref{tab:model_structures}) were fitted separately for each of the six candidate connectivity matrices, yielding $3 + (3 \times 6) = 21$ models per response variable (42 models in total for biomass and presence--absence). Competing models were compared using Akaike's Information Criterion (AIC; \citet{akaike1974}), with lower AIC values indicating better support after accounting for model complexity.

\subsubsection*{Spatial field and mesh}

The random field was approximated by the SPDE method on a finite-element mesh built with the R package \texttt{fmesher} \citep{lindgrenFmesherTriangleMeshes2025}, using a maximum inner triangle edge of 2~km (about half the estimated Mat\'ern range). Because the
Limfjorden is a narrow, branching system in which straight-line distance is a poor proxy for connection through water, we used a barrier mesh where mesh triangles
falling on land had their correlation range reduced to 10\% of the water value, so spatial correlation does not cross land \citep{bakkaNonstationaryGaussianModels2019}.

\subsubsection*{Variance partitioning}

We decomposed the variance in cockle biomass following the marginal and conditional $R^2$ framework for generalized linear mixed models \citep{nakagawa2013, nakagawa2017}. Working on the link scale, we computed three components: the variance of the fixed-effects linear predictor $\sigma^2_f$, the
variance of the spatial random field $\sigma^2_\omega$, and the observation-level (distribution) variance $\sigma^2_d$. For a Tweedie response with a log link the
last was obtained by the lognormal approximation. Marginal
$R^2 = \sigma^2_f / (\sigma^2_f + \sigma^2_\omega + \sigma^2_d)$ is the share of
variance explained by the fixed effects alone, and conditional
$R^2 = (\sigma^2_f + \sigma^2_\omega) / (\sigma^2_f + \sigma^2_\omega + \sigma^2_d)$
the share explained by the fixed effects and the spatial field together.

% To attribute the fixed-effect share to individual blocks, we split the fixed linear predictor into its gear, environment and connectivity components ($\eta^f_i = G_i + E_i + C_i$) and gave each block a share equal to its covariance with the whole fixed predictor, $\mathrm{cov}(B, \eta^f)$, divided by the total variance. Because $\mathrm{Var}(\eta^f) = \sum_B \mathrm{cov}(B,\eta^f)$, the three block shares sum exactly to the marginal $R^2$, and covariance between blocks is apportioned between them rather than left unassigned.


\subsubsection*{Software}
We fit the spatiotemporal models with the R package \texttt{sdmTMB} \citep{andersonSdmTMBPackageFast2025}, version 0.6.0.9034. sdmTMB uses automatic differentiation and the Laplace approximation from the R package TMB \citep{kristensenTMBAutomaticDifferentiation2016} and sparse matrix structures to set up the SPDE-based GMRFs from the R package fmesher \citep{lindgrenFmesherTriangleMeshes2025}. Estimation is done via maximum marginal likelihood. We confirmed that the nonlinear optimization was consistent with convergence by checking that the Hessian matrix was positive definite and the maximum absolute log-likelihood gradient with respect to fixed effects was <0.001 \citep{andersonSdmTMBPackageFast2025}.

\subsection{Space and environment account for most of the explained variation}

Space and environment explained cockle biomass; connectivity added almost nothing (Table~\ref{tab:model-selection}). Relative to the best-supported model,
dropping the spatial random field cost $\approx$1{,}124 AIC units, dropping the environmental block cost $\approx$110, and dropping connectivity cost only
$\approx$12. Closeness centrality gave the best-supported model, but which metric occupied the connectivity slot barely mattered: all six fell within 8.0 AIC units of one another, so the data cannot distinguish among them.

\input{../output/tables/tab1_model_selection.tex}

Cockles were most abundant in shallow, saline, low-energy water (Fig.~\ref{fig:coefficients}). Biomass declined with depth ($\hat{\beta} = -1.12 \pm 0.14$ per SD) and with maximum shear stress ($-1.52 \pm 0.18$), and rose with salinity ($1.39 \pm 0.44$). Oxygen had a weaker
negative effect ($-0.54 \pm 0.24$), and temperature was indistinguishable from zero ($0.15 \pm 0.36$). Beyond these gradients, residual spatial structure decayed
over a Mat\'ern range of 5.4~km, roughly two to three grid cells.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{fig2_coeff.png}
    \caption{Standardized coefficients of the full Space + Environment + Connectivity biomass density
models (Tweedie, log link).}
    \label{fig:coefficients}
\end{figure}

Variance partitioning reinforced the same ranking (Fig.~\ref{fig:variance}). Averaged across connectivity metrics, the fixed effects explained 22.5\% of the
variance (marginal $R^2$), and the fixed effects together with the spatial field explained 84.4\% (conditional $R^2$). In the closeness centrality model, the spatial field accounted for 64.9\% of total variance, environment for 15.1\%, gear type for 3.2\% and connectivity for 2.9\%, leaving 13.8\% unexplained. These shares were stable across all six metrics: spatial field 60.3--64.9\%, environment 15.1--17.0\%, gear type 3.2--4.9\%, and connectivity 0.9--2.9\%.

\subsection{Connectivity effects are small and negative}

All six connectivity metrics entered the biomass model with negative coefficients whose confidence intervals excluded zero (Fig.~\ref{fig:coefficients}): closeness centrality $\hat{\beta} = -0.84$ (95\% CI $-1.30$, $-0.39$), in-degree $-0.63$ ($-1.15$, $-0.10$), eigenvector
centrality $-0.53$ ($-0.90$, $-0.16$), in-strength $-0.41$ ($-0.63$, $-0.18$), biomass (log) in-strength $-0.39$ ($-0.62$, $-0.16$), and presence in-strength
$-0.38$ ($-0.61$, $-0.16$). The presence model behaved the same way (in-strength $-0.31$; $-0.52$, $-0.11$; Fig.~\ref{fig:coefficients_presence}). Higher modelled larval supply therefore predicted lower biomass, not higher.

This negative association was not an artefact of how connectivity was defined: the environmental coefficients barely moved as the metric was rotated (Fig.~\ref{fig:coefficients}). It was, however, small. At 0.9--2.9\% of total variance, connectivity was the smallest explained component in every model, below even the survey-type intercept (Fig.~\ref{fig:variance}).


\subsection{The connectivity effect is conditional, not marginal}

Connectivity had almost no marginal association with cockle biomass. Pooled Spearman correlations ranged from $-0.081$ (in-strength) to $0.024$ (in-degree), so $|\rho| \le 0.081$ for every metric. The negative coefficients reported above therefore appear only after conditioning on the environmental covariates and the spatial field, and describe a partial rather than an overall association.

Connectivity was also not independent of the environmental predictors (Fig.~\ref{fig:conn_environment}). In-strength correlated with depth ($\rho = 0.59$), salinity ($\rho = -0.67$) and maximum shear stress
($\rho = -0.57$), and the remaining metrics behaved similarly (depth $\rho = 0.51$ to $0.63$; salinity $-0.43$ to $-0.75$; shear stress $-0.49$ to $-0.57$). Stations receiving more modelled larval supply were deeper and fresher, conditions under which cockles were less abundant, but also less energetic, where they were more abundant; the gradients therefore do not align in a single direction.

The resulting collinearity was moderate. Variance inflation factors for the connectivity term ranged from 1.72 to 2.32 across the six metrics, implying that
42--57\% of each metric's variance is shared with the environmental block. This is well below conventional thresholds of concern and leaves the blocks
separable, but it means the connectivity coefficient is estimated from a restricted portion of the covariate space and is sensitive to which environmental terms are included.


\subsection{Sensitivity to the dispersal year and to the spatial field}

The result did not depend on which year of the dispersal simulation was used. Recomputing every metric from each annual flow matrix (2010--2016) and correlating it with observed biomass gave correlations spanning only 0.087--0.137 across years, with the pooled matrix used in the fitted models lying inside that band for every metric (Fig.~\ref{fig:year_sensitivity}). Closeness centrality was the most stable metric across years and eigenvector centrality the least.

Dependence on the spatial field was more consequential
(Fig.~\ref{fig:space_confounding}). Refitting without the field left the sign of in-strength ($-0.41 \rightarrow -0.30$), biomass (log) in-strength ($-0.39 \rightarrow -0.34$), presence in-strength ($-0.38 \rightarrow -0.34$) and eigenvector centrality ($-0.53 \rightarrow -0.44$) unchanged, with magnitudes shrinking by 10--25\%. For in-degree ($-0.63 \rightarrow +0.30$) and closeness centrality ($-0.84 \rightarrow +0.36$), however, the coefficient reversed sign. The two purely topological metrics are therefore not separable from broad spatial structure, whereas the supply-weighted metrics are. Dropping the field also redistributed variance dramatically, with the survey-gear share rising from 3--5\% to 44--45\% and environment from 15--17\% to 26--30\% (Fig.~\ref{fig:variance_nospace}), indicating that gear and location are partly aliased in this survey and that the spatial field is what separates them.
